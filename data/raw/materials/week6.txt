Origin of geothermal energy

 Geothermal heat originates from three principal sources: (i) primordial heat

     from when the Earth was formed, (ii) heat generated by the continuous
     decay of radioactive isotopes in the Earth's crust, and (iii) solar radiation
     that is absorbed by the ground and distributed via natural groundwater
     systems and artificial structures, such as flooded coal mines.

Geothermal power generation

 Dry steam power plants draw from underground resources of steam. The

     steam is piped directly from underground wells to the power plant, where it
     is directed into a turbine/generator unit.

                       NPTEL

                                                                                                                                     12

       (Image courtesy of IRENA)
Geothermal power generation

 In flash steam power plants, extremely hot water flows up through wells in

     the ground under its own pressure. Due to differences in pressure, some of
     the hot water boils into steam, which is then used to power a
     turbine/generator. Any leftover water and condensed steam are injected
     back into the reservoir.

                       NPTEL

                                                                                                                                     13

       (Image courtesy of IRENA)
Geothermal power generation

 Binary cycle plants uses heat from low- and intermediate-temperature

     geothermal fluids (generally from 70 to 170°C) to boil a working fluid,
     usually an organic compound with a low boiling point (such as isopentane
     or isobutene). The working fluid is then vaporized in a heat exchanger,
     drives a turbine, and is finally air cooled or condensed with water.
