· A: 10 dB = 10 log 10^1 = 1*10 log 10
· B: 20 dB = 10 log 10^2 = 2*10 log 10 = B is 10x louder than A

Measuring noise

· Sound meters are designed to mimic human response to sound; they are called
  sound level meters (physical and psychological) and not sound pressure meters
  (physical) for that reason

· Human response to sound is a combination of physical and psychological
  responses
     · A purely physical measurement would not be of much use to humans

· Basic parts are microphone, amplifier, weighting network and display in dB

· Weighting network = electronic filtering circuits built into sound meters to
  cancel or attenuate certain frequencies

· Every phone has all the requirements for measuring sound levels

· Sound level meter apps for phones are available on the Internet and you can
  measure sound

Weighting networks

· 3 internationally accepted weighting scales (weighting networks) to account for
  psychological responses to sound (Sincero and Sincero, 1996):
     · A-network: approximates human response to low intensity sound; low frequencies
        are removed severely
     · B-network: approximates human response to medium intensity sound; low
        frequencies are removed moderately
     · C-network: approximates human response to high intensity sound; negligible removal
        of low frequencies

Problem

A new type 2 sound level meter is to be tested with two pure tone sources that emit 90
dB. The pure tones are at 1000 Hz and 100 Hz. Estimate the expected readings on the A, B,
and C weighting networks.

Solution

From the figure in the previous slide, we note that the relative response (correction
factor) for each of the weighting networks is zero for 1000 Hz. Thus for the pure tone
at 1000 Hz we would expect the readings on the A, B, and C networks to be 90 dB.

For 100 Hz in the same figure, the relative response for each weighting network
differs. For the A network, the meter will subtract 19.1 dB from the actual reading, for
the B network, the meter will subtract 5.6 dB from the actual reading, and for the C
network, the meter will subtract 0.3 dB. Thus, the anticipated readings would be

          A network: 90-19.1 =70.9, or 71 dB(A)
          B network: 90-5.6 = 84.4, or 84 dB(B)
          C network: 90-0.3= 89.7, or 90 dB(C)

 Problems

· Determine the sound level in dB for two identical sources of the
  following sound pressure levels with reference to 20 µPa: 40, 60, 80 dB.
    · Solution: For two sources at sound levels of 40 dB each, the sound
       pressure level with reference to 20 µPa is 3 dB higher, i.e, 43 dB.
       Similarly, for 60 dB, it will be 63 dB, for 80 dB, it will be 83 dB.

· Solve the same problem for 3 identical sources of the same sound
  pressure levels. What difference does adding multiple sources of the
  same sound pressure level make?
    · Solution: For three sources at sound levels of 40 dB each, the
       sound pressure level with reference to 20 µPa is 3.15 dB higher, i.e,
       43.15 dB. Similarly, for 60 dB, it will be 63.15 dB, for 80 dB, it will be
       83.15 dB.

     · If hearing loss is bad, the sound may have to be very loud to trigger a reflex,
        or it may not trigger the reflex at all.

       · Sometimes people can have a combination of both sensorineural and conductive hearing

          component in addition.

  Example 15-5 (Davis and Masten, 2009)

Consider the problem of a speaker in a quiet zone who wishes to
speak to a listener operating a 4.5-Mg truck 6.0 m away. The sound
level in the truck cab is about 73 dBA.

Solution
Using Figure 15-18, we can see that she is going to have to shout
very loudly to be heard. However, if she moved to within about 1.0
m, she would be able to use her "expected" voice level, that is, the
unconscious slight rise in voice level that one would normally use in
a noisy situation.
It can be seen that at distances not uncommon in living rooms or
classrooms (4.5-6.0 m), the A-weighted background level must be
below about 50 dB for normal conversation.

Day-night average noise levels

The Ldn Concept
The Ldn is the Leq computed over a 24-hour period with a "penalty" of 10 dBA for
a designated night-time period. Thus, it is a day-night average and the subscript

"dn" is assigned instead of "eq." In applications to airport noise, the Ldn may be
referred to as DNL. The night-time period is from 10 P.M. to 7 A.M. The Ldn
equation is derived from the Leq equation with the time increment specified as
1 second. Because the time over which the Ldn is computed is one day, the total
time period is 86,400 seconds. Equation 15­15 is then written as
