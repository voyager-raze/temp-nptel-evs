What is air pollution?

 Air pollution is the presence of one or more substances (indigenous or

      foreign) in the air at a concentration or for a duration that can potentially
      cause a range of adverse effects on the wellbeing of humans, animals,
      plants, and the environment, or unreasonably interferes with the comfort
      or convenience of living and/or ability to perform activities of daily living.

                       NPTEL

                                                                                                                                     05
What is an air pollutant?

 An air pollutant is any substance found in the air that is not part of its

      natural composition, or any substance whose concentration is higher
      than that in the air's natural composition.

Types of air pollution

 Two types ­ outdoor (ambient air) and indoor (household).

 Outdoor air pollution is the deterioration in ambient air quality due to

  emissions from natural and anthropogenic sources, including

  transportation, power generation, industrial activity, biomass burning,
  and domestic heating and cooking. The concentration of pollutants in

  EL outdoor air varies widely in space and time, due to the diversity of
  T sources and the influence of atmospheric processes.
  NP Indoor air pollution is the degradation of the quality of air that is present

  in confined spaces (including homes, apartments, offices, car interiors,

  schools, hospitals, museums, public transport, and other such areas) by

  harmful chemicals and other materials originating mainly from building

  materials, unvented combustion appliances, and lifestyle habits.

Non-criteria air pollutants

 Non-criteria air pollutants, also called hazardous air pollutants (HAPs) or

     toxic air pollutants (TAPs), are a defined set of airborne pollutants
     (except criteria pollutants) that may pose an immediate or potential
     hazard to human health.

  Emissions of HAPs are ubiquitous, and they include organic compounds,

  L such as chlorinated hydrocarbons, dioxins, aldehydes, polycyclic
  TE aromatic hydrocarbons, and heavy metals, such as chromium, nickel,
  NP cadmium, and mercury.
 There are more than 70,000 such chemicals that are in use

  commercially and relatively little is known about their ambient

  concentrations, persistence, transport and transformation as well as their

  effects on health and the environment, many of which take decades to

  emerge.

Vertical dispersion of air pollutants

 The vertical movement of air results in vertical dispersion of pollutants.

     Conversely, the absence of vertical dispersion results in increasing
     ground level concentrations and a larger area of impact.

 The vertical dispersion potential (the stability) of the atmosphere is

  determined by comparing the actual lapse rate against the adiabatic

  L lapse rate.
  E As a parcel of air in the Earth's atmosphere rises through the
  T atmosphere, it experiences decreasing pressure and thus expands. This
  NP expansion lowers the temperature of the air parcel, and therefore the air
  cools as it rises.

 The atmospheric lapse rate is the change of an atmospheric variable

     with a change of altitude, the variable being temperature unless
     specified otherwise (if the lapse rate is positive, the temperature
     decreases with height; conversely if negative, the temperature increases

     with height ­ temperature inversion).

Temperature inversion

 Temperature inversion, also called thermal inversion, is the reversal of

      the normal behaviour of temperature in the troposphere, in which a layer
      of cool air at the surface is overlain by a layer of warmer air. There are
      three kinds of inversions: radiation, subsidence, and frontal.

                       NPTEL

                                                                                                                                     14

        (Image courtesy of EduRev)
Types of temperature inversion

 Radiation inversion, also called ground inversion or surface temperature

      inversion, occurs when air is cooled by contact with a colder surface until
      it becomes cooler than the overlying atmosphere; this occurs most often
      on clear nights, when the ground cools off rapidly by radiation.

                       NPTEL

Air quality index

 The air quality index (AQI) is a simple, unitless index to communicate air

     quality information to the general public in a timely manner. It is a
     measure of how air pollution affects one's health within a short time
     period.

  L The AQI is based on measurement of six different pollutant types: PM2.5
  E and PM10, O3, NO2, SO2, and CO emissions. Every day, the
  T concentrations of these pollutants are monitored at the desired location.
  NP The raw measurements then are converted into subindices for each
  pollutant using standard formulae.

 The largest sub-index across the pollutants is the AQI for that location.

 The higher the AQI value, the greater the level of air pollution and the

Local impact on air quality ­ Smog

 Smog is a complex mixture of primary and secondary air pollutants,

  including oxides of carbon, sulfur and nitrogen, ozone, hydrocarbons,

  and fine particulate matter, manifesting as a dense haze mainly in urban

  areas. Apart from reducing visibility, smog can cause a variety of

  adverse effects on human health and the environment.

What is industrial smog?

 Industrial smog is an obnoxious grayish haze, and is produced primarily

      by the burning of fossil fuels, particularly high sulfur coal. It usually forms
      in cool, humid environments, and consists mostly of SO2, suspended
      droplets of sulfuric acid, and a variety of suspended solid particles.

Mitigating industrial smog

 Flue-gas desulfurization (FGD) is a set of technologies used to remove

  SO2 from the flue gases of coal- and oil-fired power plants as well as
  emissions from municipal and medical waste incinerators and a wide

  range of industrial processes, including cement and lime kilns, metal

  smelters, petroleum refineries, glass furnaces, and sulfuric acid plants.

  EL It typically consists of a vertical and cylindrical column or tower in which
  T the exhaust gas is brought into contact with a sorbent, such as limestone
  NP or lime, that absorb and react with sulfur dioxide in the waste gas stream
  to produce salts.

 FGD systems can be classified as either wet or dry depending on the

     physical state of the sorbent, and can achieve removal efficiencies of

     between 95 and 99% for most industrial applications.

                                                                                                                                    07

 Wet FGD systems employ a slurry of pulverized limestone mixed with

water to remove SO2 from the combustion exhaust gas of power plants.
The sorbent slurry, after reacting with SO2, falls to the bottom of the
absorber tower, where it is collected and transferred to a waste handling

system.                      NPTEL

What is photochemical smog?

 Photochemical smog is a brownish-gray haze caused by the action of

      solar ultraviolet radiation on atmosphere polluted with hydrocarbons,
      volatile organic compounds (VOCs) and oxides of nitrogen. It contains
      mainly ozone, nitric acid, aldehydes, and peroxyacetyl nitrate, which are
      trapped near the ground by temperature inversion.

 Transboundary haze refers to haze pollution whose physical origin is

     situated wholly or in part within the area under the national jurisdiction of
     a country and is transported into the area under the jurisdiction of
     another nation.

  It is the result of slash and burn and/or other types of moderate to large-

  L scale burning to clear land for growing agricultural crops like corn and
  E plantation trees such as pulp wood, rubber or oil palm.
  NPT The transboundary haze pollution has been an almost annual
  occurrence in Southeast Asia since the late 1990s. It usually occurs

  during the southwest monsoon season between June and September

  and becomes more severe during periods of dry weather.

 The affected countries include Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, and

  Brunei, and to a lesser extent Thailand, Vietnam and the Philippines.             05

Halogen source gases vs. ozone-depleting substances

 Ozone-depleting substances (ODSs) are a subset of halogen source gases, manufactured for specific

industrial uses or consumer products. These gases bring chlorine and bromine atoms to the stratosphere,

where they destroy ozone in chemical reactions. Important examples are the chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs),

once used in almost all refrigeration and air conditioning systems, and the halons, which were used as fire

extinguishing agents.  NPTEL

                                                                                                                               10

(Image courtesy of NOAA Chemical Sciences Laboratory)
Conversion of halogen source gases

 Halogen-containing gases present in the stratosphere can be divided into two groups: halogen source gases

and reactive halogen gases. The source gases, which include ozone-depleting substances (ODSs), are

emitted at Earth's surface by natural processes and by human activities. Once in the stratosphere, the halogen

source gases chemically convert at different rates to form the reactive halogen gases. The conversion occurs

in the stratosphere instead of the troposphere for most gases because solar ultraviolet radiation is more
                              NPTEL
intense in the stratosphere.

Effects of ozone depletion

 The depletion of stratospheric ozone leads to an increase in solar

  ultraviolet radiation at Earth's surface. The increase occurs primarily in

  the ultraviolet-B (UV-B) (280 to 315 nm) component of the Sun's

  radiation. Exposure to UV-B radiation can harm humans, other life

  forms, and materials. Most of the effects of sunlight on the human body

  are caused by UV-B radiation. A principal effect is skin erythema, which

  L leads to sunburn. Excess exposure may lead to skin cancer.
  E Overexposure to UV-B reduces
  T the size, productivity, and quality
  NP of many crop plants (rice,
  soybeans, winter wheat, cotton,

  and corn). In addition, it impairs

  the productivity of phytoplankton

  in aquatic ecosystems.
