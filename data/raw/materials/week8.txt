                                pH

Ø A critical water quality parameter that determines the usability of water.
Ø It is defined as the negative logarithm of the activity of hydrogen ions.

           pH = -[log H+] where [log H+] is the concentration of protons
Ø Hydrogen electrodes are used as an absolute standard for measuring pH
Ø In the dissociation of pure water with measurements using an H2

    electrode, the activity of protons is 10-7 mol/L
                     H2O  H+ + OH-

Ø The dissociation constant (Kw) for water is derived from this reaction,
    Kw = [H+] * [OH-]/ [H2O] = 10-14 (pKw = 14) at 25 °C and the activity
    of H2O is 1.0

Ø For drinking water, pH should be between 6.5 to 8.5.

Ø It measures the concentrations of all cations and anions present in water.      Ionic strength
Ø It is a property of the solution, not of individual ions.
Ø Was first defined by Lewis and Randall in 1921.

                     I = ½  CiZi2
where Ci = concentration of any ion i in moles/L; Zi = charge of any ion i
Ø Ionic strength calculations require complete characterization of the water

   sample for each individual cation and anion which can be done using
   instruments like ion chromatograph.
Ø Ionic strength can also be estimated by multiplying the Total Dissolved Solids
   by 2.5 x 10-5 based on Langelier's method.
                  Electrical Conductivity (EC)

Ø It is a measure of the concentration of all ions present in water;
Ø Units are micro-Siemens/cm (µS/cm)
Ø The inverse of EC is electrical resistivity (ER) which has units of Mega-ohms-

    cm (M/cm).
Ø Both values are generally reported for 25 °C.
Ø It is a direct and inexpensive measure of the ionic strength of a solution
Ø Conductivity serves as a convenient surrogate parameter for Total Dissolved

    Solids (TDS) which is a difficult and time-consuming parameter to measure

    as per standard methodology.
Ø Conductivity can be measured in the field instantaneously with a portable

    (pocket-size) probe.
Ø Conductivity and TDS are directly proportionate and can be expressed as:

                     TDS (mg/L) = A* Conductivity

      where, A = conversion factor ranging from 0.55 to 0.75 (mg-cm/ L-S)
Ø Conductivity is linearly related to ionic strength in dilute solutions with I 

    0.1 M.
Ø Total solids includes all solids, dissolved and suspended                         Solids
Ø Total solids are further divided into a few categories
Ø Total Suspended Solids (TSS): For water quality measurement, suspended

    solids are defined as solids that are separated using a membrane filter of 1.5
    or 1.7 micron pore size.
Ø Total Dissolved Solids (TDS): The solids that pass through the above
    mentioned filter, i.e., solids remaining in the filtrate are defined as TDS.
Ø Total Fixed Solids (TFS): When total solids are burned at 550 °C in a muffle
    furnace, the residue remaining after burning is defined as TFS.
Ø Total Volatile Solids (TVS): The difference in weight between total solids and
    total fixed solids is defined as TVS.

                                        TS = TFS + TVS
Ø Fixed Suspended Solids (FSS): When TSS are burned at 550 °C in a muffle

    furnace, the residue remaining after burning is defined as FSS.
Ø Volatile Suspended Solids (VSS): The difference in weight between TSS and

    FSS defines volatile suspended solids.

                                       TSS = FSS + VSS

Ø Dissolved oxygen levels, the health and biodiversity of any ecosystem, the      Temperature
   extent and rate of all physical, chemical and biological processes are
   temperature-dependent.

Ø Temperature determines the fate and transport of contaminants in water
Ø Factors influencing water temperature for unpolluted surface water bodies

   are: depth of the water column, incident light intensity, turbidity of water,
   and ambient temperature.
Ø Temperature affects water quality parameters like the density of water,
   dissolved oxygen concentrations, solubility and/or dissolution of various
   contaminants, growth of organisms, and volatilization of contaminants.
Ø Reaction rates, equilibrium constants and therefore, speciation of various
   contaminants at equilibrium depend on temperature.
Ø Turbidity indicates the presence of light-scattering substances in water                                                     Turbidity
Ø It is associated with particles which may be substances like clay and silt, or

   biological particles like bacteria or algae.
Ø High turbidity levels preclude light from penetrating deep into the water

   column and result in a shallow photic (light) zone.
Ø Photosynthesis organisms are capable of growing only in the photic zone

   since the aphotic (darker) zone cannot sustain their growth.
Ø This results in the lack of oxygen generation in the aphotic zone; therefore

   low to zero DO levels in this zone.
Ø A turbidity meter or nephelometer is used for measuring turbidity and is

   based on measuring the intensity of light either transmitted or scattered by
   a sample, respectively.
Ø Both instruments are compared to standard solutions of known turbidity for
   calibration.
Ø The intensity of scattered light is a measure of the sample's turbidity and is
   measured in nephelometric turbidity units (NTU).

Ø It is a measure of the capacity of water to neutralize acids.                            Alkalinity
Ø The most common contributors to alkalinity in water are bicarbonates, carbonates,

   and hydroxides which remove H+ ions and lower the acidity of the water, i.e.,
   increase its pH.
Ø Measuring alkalinity is important in determining a stream's ability to neutralize

   acidic pollution from rainfall or wastewater.
Ø Total alkalinity is measured by measuring the amount of acid needed to bring

   the sample to a pH of 4.5 (APHA, AWWA, WEF 2005).
Ø At this pH, all alkaline compounds in the sample are consumed in the neutralization

   reaction.
Ø The result is reported as milligrams per liter of calcium carbonate (mg/ L as CaCO3).
Ø Hydroxide alkalinity is significant only when the sample pH is high, i.e., > 11, and is

   directly determined based on pH and Kw.
Ø Samples containing only carbonates and bicarbonates, and insignificant amounts of

   hydroxides generally have 8.3  pH  11. In that case,

            Bicarbonate alkalinity = Total alkalinity ­ carbonate alkalinity
Ø Samples containing bicarbonates alone have pH  8.3 and

            Bicarbonate alkalinity = Total alkalinity
Ø Hardness in water is attributed to the presence of divalent cations like Ca and Mg.    Hardness
Ø The presence of these cations leads to various problems in water supply systems:
· Formation of soap curd (white precipitate of soap) and lack of frothing
· Scale formation or precipitation of hardness-contributing ions, CaCO3 and Mg(OH)2
Ø Hardness is expressed in terms of mg/ L as CaCO3.
Ø The desirable limit for total hardness (TH) is 200 mg/ L and the permissible limit in

   the absence of another source is 600 mg/ L.
Ø Total hardness (TH) = [Ca2+] + [Mg2+], expressed as mg/L of CaCO3.
Ø Carbonate Hardness (CH) = Hardness associated with carbonate and bicarbonate ions.
Ø Non-carbonate Hardness (NCH) = Difference between Total Hardness and Carbonate

   Hardness = TH ­ CH
Ø If the concentrations of carbonate and bicarbonate ions are greater than TH,

   then TH = CH and NCH = 0.
Ø Carbonate Hardness is also called `temporary hardness' since it can be removed by

   heating.
Ø Color in water can be due to the presence of dissolved or particulate matter            Color
   that may be either organic or inorganic.

Ø Organic matter includes dissolved compounds like humic and fulvic acids
   which give water a light tea-like yellow color and particulate matter like
   phytoplankton which impart a green or yellow color to water.

Ø Inorganic matter includes dissolved metals like iron, manganese and
   chromium which causes water to have colors ranging from yellow to red.

Ø Metals like iron and manganese precipitate slowly as they get oxidised in
   contact with atmospheric oxygen leading to colored precipitates red or
   black.

Ø Measured in terms of Hazen units, platinum-cobalt units or APHA-Hazen
   units

Ø The Pt-Co scale is specific to the color yellow and is based on dilutions of a
   500 ppm platinum cobalt solution. The colour produced by one milligram of
   platinum cobalt dissolved in one liter of water is fixed as one unit of color on
   the platinum-cobalt scale.

Three biochemical pathways used by bacteria to survive and reproduce                 Bacteria

· Aerobic respiration
     · measured in terms of heterotrophic plate count (HPC) bacteria
     · The terminal electron acceptor is oxygen for aerobic bacteria
     · C6H12O6 + 6O2 ® 6CO2 + 6 H2O
     · The 6C atoms in glucose donate all their electrons in the complete
        oxidation of C in CO2 as the oxidation state of C in glucose goes from 0 to
        +4, i.e., 24 e- are donated.
     · These 24 e- are accepted by O2 which as a reactant had an oxidation
        state of 0 which is then converted to -2 in CO2 and H2O

· Anaerobic respiration
     · Terminal electron acceptors can be any oxidized species like Fe(III),
        Mn(IV), CO3-, NO3- except oxygen
     · Facultative bacteria can switch electron acceptors depending on change
        in nutrient availability or environmental conditions.

· Fermentative
     · Bacteria that can utilize organic compounds as both electron donor and
        electron acceptor such as coliforms
     · C6H12O6 ® 3CO2 + 3CH4

Factors affecting the DO sag curve                                                        Dissolved
Ø Seasonal changes: During summer, due to decrease in water level,                        oxygen sag

   wastewater concentration increases, resulting in higher BOD concentration in              curve
   the river or stream, thereby bringing down DO levels.
Ø Temperature effect: With an increase in temperature, wastewater
   decomposition is faster. Thus, rate of deoxygenation increases and DO level
   decreases. The critical time is achieved sooner in summer compared to winter
   due to difference in water temperature.
Ø Effect of photosynthesis: Photosynthetic organisms like algae and aquatic
   plants add DO during the daytime, and consume DO during night time due to
   respiration.
Ø Nitrification: Another secondary dip in DO sag curve is observed downstream
   of the discharge point due to conversion of organic nitrogen and ammonia to
   nitrites and nitrates.
Ø Wastewater concentration: As wastewater concentration increases, the
   amount of DO consumed also increases leading to greater depletion in DO
   levels.
