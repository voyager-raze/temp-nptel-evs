The environmental movement has also been marked by several very important
publications. As far back as 1798, Thomas Malthus in the UK published a book called An
Essay on the Principle of Population. He was talking about rampant exponential population
growth and the impact it would have on food production as well as the demographics of
society in the future. Malthusian economics has had a tremendous influence on our thinking
even to this day and it's also responsible or basically it has influenced the thinking of
economists, scientists and so many other disciplines about the long-term impacts of
rampant exponential population growth. So, I will say a little bit more about that in the next
points. In 1913, Rabindranath Tagore in India published the Gitanjali and several other
books and all of his works show the connection between human beings' nature and
spirituality. In 1949, Aldo Leopold published a book, The Sandy County Almanac. It's
basically around the theme of the moral and ethical imperative in conserving nature. It's
important for everyone to remember that prior to many of these publications and the public
debate and discussion that followed, the reigning paradigm at that time was that nature and
natural resources are meant for the consumption of human beings, that human beings do
not have any need to take care of nature. So, these works especially as Rabindranath
Tagore, Aldo Leopold, they are all about the moral, ethical and spiritual requirements for
maintaining and conserving nature. 1962, Rachel Carson who was a marine biologist
published the book, The Silent Spring among other books, but this particular book was a

woke up to the damage that pesticides and other organic compounds were causing to

for the environmental movement and I have already explained the IPAT model in earlier

We will start with the Bishnoi movement. The Bishnoi people are a sect that lives in the
western Thar desert and other parts of northern India. They are followers of Guru
Jambeshwar who was alive in this region from 1451 to 1536. One of the major principles
of this group of people is to support wildlife protection, eco conservation and green living.
In September of 1730, Jodhpur's king Abhay Singh had given his men orders to clear the
trees in a village called Khejarli for wood. So, these men were sent to this particular village
in September 1730. Amrita Devi, her family and other local people from this village stood
in opposition to the king's men and as a sign of protest they hugged the trees to prevent the
men from cutting the trees down. However, 363 Bishnois were killed in the protest until
the king was informed about it and he revoked his order and stopped the killings. So, this
was as far back as 1730.
Then came the Chipko movement in 1973. The first Chipko protest occurred in the village
of Mandal in upper Alaknanda valley in the month of April in 1973. The local villagers
were denied access to trees in a small area for making agricultural tools. At the same time
the government allotted a much larger plot including that area which the villagers wanted
access to. That larger plot was allotted to a sporting goods manufacturer for logging of
trees. When their appeals were denied, a Gandhian social worker Chandi Prasad Bhatt led
villagers into the forest and again similar to the Bishnoi movement they embraced the trees
to prevent the loggers from moving in. After many days of protest the government
cancelled the company's logging permit and the original allotment which was requested by
Chandi Prasad Bhatt was given to his organization Dasholi Gram Swarajya Sangh. So, this
was a sort of happy ending. As the movement continued the protest became more project
oriented. They expanded to include all of the region. So what is today Uttarakhand and
Northern UP, Northwestern UP, all of this was included in this movement. It ultimately
became the Save Himalaya movement. Between 1981 and 1983 Sundarlal Bahuguna
marched 5000 km across the Himalayas to popularise this particular movement.
Throughout the 1980s this group of people protested against the Tehri dam on the
Bhagirathi River as well as mining operations in this area. The consequence was the closure
of at least one limestone quarry. A massive reforestation effort was made that led to the
planting of more than 1 million trees in this particular region. In 1987 the Chipko
movement was awarded the right livelihood award for its dedication to the conservation,
restoration and ecologically sound use of India's natural resources.
We now come to another movement in the eastern part of India. This movement is called
the Jangal Bachao Andolan which started in 1979. The aim of this movement again was to
stop the government from replacing the natural Sal Forest. So, you have salwood trees and
teakwood trees. The natural forest was Sal and the government wanted to replace it with
teak trees. The tribals of the Singhbhum district of Bihar started the protest. For them the
sal tree was considered sacred and provided leaves, nuts and firewood while teak was going
to be sold by the government for profit leaving nothing for the tribals. So, it is
understandable that the tribals were up in arms because they were going to lose their source
of livelihood. Later this movement spread to Orissa and Jharkhand as well.
We now come to the last of the movements that I am going to be talking about and that is
the Apiko movement in 1983. Apiko is similar to the Chipko movement in the north but
this one was centered in Karnataka. It was started by environmental activist Pandurang
Hegde and the aim of the movement was to conserve jackfruit trees in Karnataka. The
youth and women of Saklani village and its surroundings walked around five miles to the
nearby forest and they hugged the trees there in a similar form of protest as the Chipko
movement. Their demand was to ban the felling of trees and their protest continued for
about 38 days until the state government withdrew the order for felling of the trees. Like
the Chipko movement, the Apiko movement also chose the Gandhian way of protest to
achieve a sustainable society in which human beings and nature are in balance.
