So, what we have here is the definition of e-waste.

E-waste is the abbreviated form for electrical and electronic waste. It is also termed
WEEE which stands for waste, electrical and electronic equipment. So, any obsolete or
discarded electrical and electronic items are referred to as e-waste. There are 6 categories
of equipment that are used to classify e-waste

The first one is temperature exchange equipment. All the cooling and freezing equipment
that we use refrigerators, freezers, air conditioners and heat pumps come in this first
category. The second category screens, monitors and equipment containing screens that
includes televisions, your computer monitors, laptops, tablets and so on. Category 3 is
light providing light emitting devices. So, fluorescent lamps, LED lamps all of them
come in category 3.

Category 4 we have large equipment. So, washing machines, washing clothes dryers,
dish washing machines, large printing devices, photo-voltage panels they all come in
category 4. Category 5 includes small household equipment. So, vacuum cleaners,
microwaves, ventilation equipment, toasters, kettles, electric kettles, shavers, scales,
electrical toys, small medical devices, control instruments all these are category 5. Small
information technology and telecom equipment, mobile phones, personal computers,
printers, game, consoles, calculators all of them are category 6.

Let me start with international agreements. One of the most important international
agreement which has an impact on global e-waste management is the Basel Convention.
The Basel Convention was brought into effect to prevent the transboundary import and
export of e-waste basically from developed countries to developing countries.

So, then we come to how can we manage e-waste properly. The first step is rules and
regulations and I have already mentioned the extended producer responsibility. There are

One is take-back. So, as part of the extended producer responsibility, it is the
responsibility of the manufacturer to take back whatever they have produced that is
hazardous in nature and the best example that comes to mind is lead acid batteries.

  So, those of you who are familiar with lead acid batteries which are used in motor

which need to be treated in the same way. The second option here is exchange. You can
exchange the e-waste for either money or some kind of financial incentive.

Many places are providing coupons and so on to incentivize purchase of other materials
in exchange for e-waste that is one scheme that has come up and it is been fairly

The recovery of ferrous and non-ferrous metals can be done in four different ways. So,
we have four categories of methods that are available these days. Developed countries
have relied on pyrometallurgical techniques. Now pyrometallurgical techniques rely on
high temperature processes for extracting the metal and purifying it and these are
extremely energy intensive processes.

So, these energy intensive processes are hazardous for the environment. There are high
amounts of carbon dioxide emissions and the cost is also very high. We are exploring in
India, we are exploring, we do not have pyrometallurgy in India, we are exploring
hydrometallurgy, bio metallurgy and electrometallurgy in India for extracting metals from
e-waste. Hydrometallurgy and biometallurgy and to some extent electrometallurgy are
considered environmentally friendly methods in comparison to pyrometallurgy. So, these
processes mainly considered poor because of greenhouse gas emissions and the energy
required for running these processes.
So, these three processes hydrometallurgy, biometallurgy, electrometallurgy these are
promising areas for research and development and there are people working on these
areas for extracting both rare earth metals as well as ferrous and non-ferrous metals.
