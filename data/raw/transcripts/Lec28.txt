We know that solar energy, or irradiation from the sun, is absorbed by the ocean and stored
as thermal energy in the upper layers. Ocean Thermal Energy Conversion (OTEC)
technologies capitalize on the temperature difference between the warm surface water and
the cold deep-sea layers at depths of 800 to 1000 meters, converting this thermal energy
into electricity, heat, or cold through a thermal cycle. For an OTEC system to function
effectively, the temperature difference must be around 20 degrees Celsius. This means the
surface temperature needs to be approximately 25 degrees Celsius, as the deep-sea water
temperature remains relatively constant at about 4 degrees Celsius at a depth of 1000
meters. This temperature difference between the cold deep water and warm surface water
is crucial for OTEC technologies to operate. Both onshore and offshore OTEC plants are
feasible, as shown in the diagram. However, like tidal energy, the specific conditions
necessary for ocean thermal energy production are only present in certain regions of the
world.
This technology is particularly effective in the tropical regions between latitudes of
approximately 30 degrees north and 30 degrees south. These areas benefit from warm
surface water temperatures, significant water depths, and stable temperatures throughout
the year. Despite being restricted to the tropics, the global technical potential of OTEC is
the highest among all ocean energy resources, offering around 44,000 terawatt-hours per
year of steady-state power. One of the key advantages of OTEC technology is its ability to
provide non-intermittent, continuous base load power around the clock, unlike many other
renewable energy technologies like solar and wind, which are highly intermittent.

OTEC's ability to consistently generate power stems from the stable temperature difference
between the surface and deep ocean waters, allowing for uninterrupted renewable energy
production. Additionally, OTEC can be integrated with other technologies, such as
seawater reverse osmosis, to produce freshwater. The waste water from electricity
generation can also be repurposed for aquaculture, creating additional revenue streams and
business opportunities, particularly in tropical islands where such applications are highly
relevant.
At present, three prominent conversion processes are used: open cycle, closed cycle, and
hybrid devices. In an open-cycle OTEC technology, as depicted in the accompanying
image, warm surface water is flash-evaporated under low pressure to serve as the working
fluid. The resulting vapour is then used to drive a turbine, which powers a generator to
produce electricity. Cold deep-sea water is subsequently used to condense the vapour,
generating desalinated water as a byproduct. This system can also bypass electricity
generation entirely, using the process for freshwater production through a low-temperature
thermal desalination system. The cold seawater used for condensation can be repurposed
for cooling or is well-suited for aquaculture.
In contrast, closed-cycle OTEC technology does not evaporate the warm water itself.
Instead, the warm water is used to flash evaporate another working fluid with a lower
boiling point than water, such as ammonia, in a heat exchanger. In the closed cycle OTEC
system, the working fluid--such as ammonia--is flash evaporated and the resulting vapor
drives a turbo generator to produce electricity. The ammonia vapor is then condensed by
coming into contact with cold deep-sea water and is pumped back into the closed system
to repeat the cycle. This method is more efficient and is better suited for smaller
applications. Additionally, the cycle can be reversed, using the cold water to cool a working
fluid through heat exchangers to provide seawater air conditioning instead of generating
electricity, offering another versatile application of this technology.

Hybrid OTEC systems, as the name suggests, combine both open and closed cycle
technologies. In a hybrid system, the process begins with electricity generation using a
closed-loop system. However, instead of discharging the warm water afterward, it is flash
evaporated and passed through an open cycle, generating additional electricity and/or
producing desalinated water. The key advantage of this ocean thermal energy conversion
technology over other ocean energy technologies is that, in addition to generating
electricity, it also provides desalinated water, which can be repurposed for aquaculture or
other activities.
Salinity gradient energy, also known as osmotic energy, leverages the pressure potential
difference between ocean water's salt concentration and fresh water, converting it into
usable energy through membranes. A significant amount of energy--up to 0.8 kilowatts
per cubic meter--can theoretically be harvested from the salinity difference between
seawater and freshwater, which is comparable to the energy generated by water falling over
a dam more than 280 meters high. However, the limiting factor is the supply of fresh water.
Globally, about 2 terawatts of energy is available from rivers flowing into the ocean, with
around 980 gigawatts being harvestable. Additionally, wastewater discharge into the ocean
could provide another 800 gigawatts of power. Despite its potential, salinity gradient
energy remains largely untapped, requiring advanced engineering and efficient energy
conversion technologies.

The basic principle involves selective membranes that favor the transport of counter ions
from a high-concentration reservoir to a low-concentration one, generating an ionic current.
This ionic current, when converted via redox reactions at reference electrodes, produces
electric current, closing the electrical circuit. The global potential for salinity gradient
energy varies due to non-uniform ocean salinity, influenced by factors such as ice melting,
river runoff, and precipitation. Regions with significant river discharges into the sea are
particularly suitable for harnessing this energy, as the energy generated is proportional to
the salt concentration difference, making freshwater-saltwater systems highly efficient.

Estuaries, where fresh and saltwater meet, are prime locations for salinity gradient energy
plants, which could theoretically operate continuously to provide baseload power.
However, the geographical requirements pose significant limitations, making salinity
gradient energy potential relatively low, at about 1,650 terawatt-hours per year--the lowest
among ocean energy forms.

Two main processes are widely considered for harnessing salinity gradient energy: pressure
retarded osmosis (PRO) and reverse electrodialysis (RED). In PRO, a semi-permeable
membrane separates low-concentration and high-concentration solutions, allowing water
to move from low to high concentrations, increasing the volume flow rate on the saltwater
side. The resultant high-pressure solution is split, with one part driving a turbine and the
other returning to a pressure exchanger, which pressurizes the saltwater feed, generating
electricity.

In RED, perm-selective membranes replace semi-permeable ones, with fresh and saltwater
flowing through alternating channels. These membranes transfer cations and anions from
high to low-concentration solutions, creating an electrochemical potential difference that
generates an electrical current at the electrodes. The energy that can be extracted from
mixing a dilute stream with a saline stream is significant, but membranes remain a critical
component and technical bottleneck for these plants. These membranes must be highly
efficient ion rectifiers with high ion flux and long-term robustness in seawater. However,
the vast quantities required are commercially unavailable, and regular replacement is
necessary, posing challenges to the widespread or full-scale application of salinity gradient
energy technology.
In addition to the ocean movement caused by rising and falling tides, ocean currents
represent another significant movement mechanism in the oceans. These large circulations
are driven by an interplay of wind, temperature, and salinity across the globe. Similar to
wind turbines, turbines can be installed underwater to harness the energy of ocean currents
and generate electricity. A key advantage of this technology is our understanding of the
locations of major ocean currents, such as the Gulf Stream in North America, the Agulhas
Current in South Africa, the Kuroshio Current in East Asia, and the Eastern Australian
Current. Although ocean currents are generally slower than tidal streams, they involve
much larger volumes of water, providing a substantial energy potential.

Another advantage of ocean currents is their unidirectional and continuous flow with
minimal fluctuations, unlike tidal streams that have bidirectional flow. This makes ocean
currents ideally suited for base load power generation. However, it's important to note that
ocean current energy technologies have not yet been extensively tested. The technologies
used to harness ocean currents are expected to be similar to those used for tidal streams,
such as horizontal axis turbines, vertical axis turbines, and possibly tidal kites that follow
an 8-shaped trajectory. Since tidal energy technologies are themselves still emerging, they
are naturally suited to operate at lower speeds. Therefore, advancements in tidal current
technologies will likely pave the way for the development of more effective ocean current
energy technologies.

Now that we've discussed the various forms of renewable energy resources available in the
ocean, it's essential to consider the potential environmental and social aspects of ocean
current energy technologies.
Ocean energy technologies offer significant promise for climate change mitigation as they
do not generate greenhouse gases during operation and have low life cycle emissions.
While they present a potential solution for global CO reduction, their impact on marine
life and the environment is not yet fully understood, primarily because these technologies
are still in the early stages of deployment. Potential negative impacts include habitat loss,
animal interactions with turbines, noise, and electromagnetic fields produced by sea cables,
all of which could affect aquatic species. However, the limited studies conducted so far are
often location-specific, making it difficult to generalize their findings. Additionally,
increased vessel traffic due to deployment and maintenance could further exacerbate
environmental impacts.

Despite these challenges, major deployment sites today are equipped with monitoring
devices to study the effects on the environment, focusing on collision risks, acoustic
impacts, and the effects on currents, erosion, and sediment transport. Encouragingly, initial
research has shown mostly positive outcomes, with underwater noise generally remaining
below the hearing threshold for most species and minimal collision risks observed for tidal
plants. However, large-scale deployments are necessary to fully understand the long-term
impacts on marine life and the environment.

From a socio-political perspective, the deployment of ocean energy technologies raises
concerns about the sharing of ocean resources. Over two-thirds of the ocean is part of the
global commons, not governed by any specific nation, leading to potential ownership
disputes among communities, countries, and sectors, particularly in areas where fishing,
conservation, shipping, and defense are already established. Additionally, for near-shore
installations, community readiness and potential visual impacts must be considered to
avoid public rejection of projects.

The deployment of ocean energy technologies has been slower than anticipated, despite the
recognized need to decarbonize the power sector. Tidal energy is nearing
commercialization, with a focus on horizontal axis turbines and the development of large-
scale, multi-turbine tidal farms. Wave energy, however, remains less mature, with
technologies still in the scale testing and demonstration stages. OTEC and salinity gradient
technologies are in the early development stages, with limited demonstration projects and
various challenges to overcome before reaching commercial scale. Currently, the
cumulative installed capacity across all ocean technologies is 534.7 megawatts, with tidal
barrage technology accounting for the majority at 521.5 megawatts. Other ocean energy
technologies contribute 13.2 megawatts, primarily from tidal streams.

Geographically, ocean energy development is concentrated in 31 countries, particularly in
European nations like Finland, France, Ireland, Italy, Portugal, Spain, Sweden, and the
United Kingdom, as well as in Australia, Canada, and the United States. These countries
lead in ocean energy project development and device manufacturing. Looking ahead, more
capacity additions are expected, with wave and tidal stream projects with a combined
capacity of 2.83 gigawatts in the pipeline as of 2020. The International Renewable Energy
Agency estimates that around 10 gigawatts could be commercially deployed by 2030.
OTEC, salinity gradient, and ocean current technologies also have significant potential and
are expected to reach a more mature stage by the end of the decade.
Despite ongoing small-scale projects, these technologies are still largely in the research
and development stages, with work primarily conducted in research institutes and
universities. In summary, ocean energy is increasingly seen as an essential component of
the solution to climate change. Due to its predictability and more stable generation
compared to other renewable energy resources, ocean energy can support and stabilize
grids that integrate variable resources like solar PV and wind. However, there is still
considerable scope for development, particularly in ocean thermal energy conversion,
salinity gradient, and ocean current technologies. While tidal and wave energy have shown
significant progress, they still need to be scaled up to larger capacities. Over time, it is
hoped that these technologies will see greater deployment and further advancements.
