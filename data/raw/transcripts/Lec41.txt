                   Importance and Integrated Solid Waste Management

Welcome everyone to week 9. We are going to be looking at solid waste management in
this week, and our first lecture is going to be about the importance of solid waste
management and what is integrated solid waste management.
In this particular lecture, I will be covering the importance of solid waste management,
the objectives and principles of integrated solid waste management. So, some of the
principles that we will look at are waste management hierarchy, the four R's, green
design policy, and standardization.

The keywords for this particular lecture are solid waste management, municipal solid
waste, generation rate, waste management hierarchy, the four R's, source reduction, and
labeling.
So, we are now going to start with solid and hazardous waste management. The first
question to ask is what is solid waste? Any waste material that is not in liquid or gaseous
state is considered to be solid waste. Now, one of the things that is very important to note
is that containerized liquid and gaseous waste, which is very frequent in your municipal
solid waste, you will find that you have cans of pesticide, you have paint cans, and so
many other things which have hazardous material or even otherwise you have containers
which contain residual liquid or gaseous material. All these containerized liquid and
gaseous waste are also included in solid waste because they end up in that category of
waste. We now look at some other categories of solid waste.

The first one, which I think everyone knows is municipal solid waste. So, all of us are
continuously generating solid waste. And our trash cans, our dust bins, we are putting all
this material into our dust bins or trash cans. This material is collected and so on and that
is the responsibility of the municipality. So, that is called municipal solid waste.
Anything coming from residential, commercial, institutional sources, we will be going
into more details later, that is all under municipal solid waste. So, within any city or town
limit, whatever is generated is municipal solid waste.

One of the biggest sources of waste is agriculture. After the harvest is done, the useful
crop is removed from the fields, and the crop residue is left behind. That is agricultural
waste, and it is a huge amount of waste because our economy has a significant input from
agriculture. Agricultural waste constitutes almost one-quarter of the total waste generated
in the country.
Thermal power plants are another major generator of solid waste. Coal powered thermal
power plants, when they utilize coal after it is burnt, there are two fractions that are
formed, and one is called bottom ash, the other is called fly ash. Now why is it called fly
ash? Because after the material has been burnt, there are fine particles that are released
along with the hot gases and they go through the chimney stack. Even with air pollution
control equipment like electrostatic precipitators, bag filters and so on, you can remove
this fine particulate matter and that is called fly ash. And it is extremely fine particulate
matter which comes into the particle size range of less than 1 micron or maybe less than
2.5 microns and so on. So this huge amount of ash that is generated by thermal power
plants is a major fraction of the total waste generated, total solid waste generated in the
country.

Another major source of solid waste is industrial and mining activities. You know that
when you are trying to remove metals from their ore, there is a huge amount of ore that is
extracted from the ground and then the metal is extracted from that mineral ore. Now
these industrial and mining activities result in a huge amount of waste material and these
are called mine tailings from the mining waste, it is called mine tailings and from
industry, it is simply called solid waste.

Now I have another category over here that is called hazardous waste. Hazardous waste is
not necessarily solid waste but in practice, it really is a fraction of solid waste. If you go
to any TSDF facility, a TSDF facility is `Treatment Storage and Disposal Facility' and
this hazardous material is generally containerized waste. So, whether it is in solid, liquid
or gaseous form, it is containerized, and because it is containerized, it comes under solid
waste. The definition of hazardous waste is also given in this box. Anything that is
reactive, ignitable, corrosive, toxic, radioactive, infective, etc. will be considered
hazardous waste, and just keep in mind that most hazardous waste is containerized, and
therefore it comes under solid waste.
Okay, so before we proceed to integrated solid waste management, let us take a look at
the hazards associated with open dumping of solid waste. So here I have a rough sketch
of an open dump. Anywhere in the country, you will be able to find these open dumps.
They are there everywhere and most cities and towns in the country have these kinds of
unsightly dumps. Now how do we recognize these dumps? The first signal that we get is
when there is a stench in the air. You know that something is degrading around you, you
look around and you find there is an open dump of waste. So, this open dump of
municipal solid waste is a common site and volatile organic compounds which are
generated by the decomposition of this waste are being, they are being scattered into the
atmosphere because of wind and they are released into the air because of wind. So that is
how we smell them. So, these are these volatile organic compounds. What else is a
common site which takes a little effort to understand and that is that if you happen to be
in large cities like Kolkata, Mumbai, Delhi and so on, there you will find that these open
dumps are a few tens of meters high. So, they are almost 20 meters in height and often
you will find that there is fire or smoke coming out of these dumps. And that is because
the decomposition of the waste that is inside the dump generates what we call methane
gas. These are anaerobic conditions. Methane gas is generated. Methane is a flammable
gas. It can ignite, it gets ignited or it burns very slowly for various reasons and you see
smoke coming out of these dumps. So, either you see the entire dump on fire. There are
several reports in the media, the internet is full of these kinds of reports where the entire
dump is on fire and that is because of methane building up in these open dumps and that
can lead to explosions or fires for that last for a few days in some cases. So, there are
many reports of this kind of air pollution.
Let us then come to water pollution. Now we have two major categories of water bodies,
surface water and groundwater. This open dump is exposed to rainwater as well as water
vapor from the atmosphere. So, because of decomposition of the waste, water is squeezed
out of the waste by compaction as well as decomposition. Now this water has to either go
as surface runoff or it has to go into the groundwater. It infiltrates into the subsurface and
it can potentially contaminate both groundwater as well as surface water. So, this is one
of the biggest problems associated with open dumping of solid waste. You have the
potential for short term as well as long term contamination of these groundwater
resources that is what we consider some of it is non-renewable resource and these
groundwater and surface water resources can be contaminated. Another issue is which I
cannot show you in this sketch, but if you understand the natural drains, there are both
natural drainage systems and engineered drainage systems that allow surface water
during a rainstorm to be collected and it goes to a water body which will be at a much
lower level of elevation. So, water flows in the downhill direction. Now if you have open
dumps that are either contributing to the clogging of these drains, either they are in the
middle of these drains and therefore they are clogging it or even small things like plastic
bags and so on which clog our engineered drainage systems are also responsible for
creating problems. So, in fact water logging in cities during the monsoon is often due to
clogging of these natural and engineered drainage systems. So that is one of the most
direct again visual effects of open dumping of waste. So, it may be as small as a single
plastic bag which may cover a drain and prevent the water from flowing in into the drain
or it may be larger open dumps which block the entire drainage system.

Then we come to soil pollution. Now soil pollution will, this open dump that I have
shown here in the rough sketch is in contact with the soil below it. Now there are several
contaminants that are present in this solid waste which will over a period of time due to
decomposition and degradation will be leached into the soil and there these contaminants
will be attached to the soil particles and the organic matter which is actually natural
organic matter but they will form complexes. So, the sequestering of contaminants in soil
can lead to soil toxicity and in the long term it will decrease the soil productivity.

We then come to the last but not the least. In fact, the last point is actually one of the
most important points. These open dumps are a serious public health hazard. They are a
breeding ground for all kinds of pestilent species. So anytime you look at an open dump
you will find that all kinds of animals, flies, rodents, mosquitoes, dogs, cats, you name it,
any kind of animal that is attracted to these dumps either for breeding or for food will
become a problem in that particular local area. So it is, these dumps will release
pathogens into other environmental media. So, into the soil, into the water and so on.
They also attract the growth of flies, rodents and other pests and in fact open dumping of
waste was considered a contributing factor to the plague in Surat in 1994. I have already
mentioned that several stray animals, dogs, cats, cows, buffaloes, all of them are attracted
to these open dumps because they see them as a source of food and because of that it
contributes to bad traffic conditions and therefore a higher accident potential. They are
also an aesthetic nuisance. I have already mentioned that visually as well as because of
the odors coming out of the decomposition of the waste, it is an aesthetic problem, and
because of that there is a loss of civic health and pride and therefore loss of property
values as well.

So, let us now come to integrated solid waste management. Keeping the background that
if you do not manage solid waste properly, we are going to be faced with problems of air
pollution, water pollution, soil pollution, public health problems. All of them are related
to poor management of solid waste. What we want is integrated solid waste management.
What are our objectives as part of integrated solid waste management?
So, I am going to start with the circle over here. What we want is to minimize public
health impacts. As I said our first priority always is to ensure that public health is not
damaged in any way because of poor management of municipal services. So that is our
first one.
Our secondary objective is to minimize environmental impacts. We also do not want to
damage our ecosystems. We do not want to damage our soil ecosystems because that is
what our productivity depends on. We do not want to damage our water resources. We do
not want to damage. Obviously, we do not want to cause air pollution and so on. So, any
damage to the ecology, the ecosystems, or to the environment has to be minimized.
We want to minimize resource consumption. In my very first lecture, I spoke about
minimizing resource consumption, and that means implementing the four Rs. We will
very quickly review the four Rs in this particular lecture.
Because of the issues of climate change, we also want to minimize greenhouse gas
emissions and we also want to minimize the use of landfills. For a very long time, people
have not been cognizant of the issues related to building landfills. We want to ensure that
minimum amount of land is utilized for building landfills. Sometimes, they are essential.
We cannot get away with it, but we also need to remember that there is a current trend
and the trend in developed countries is to have zero waste to landfills. So, in the present,
in developed countries and our future in developing countries is to have zero landfills.
Our goal for the future is no more landfills.

What I have in this slide is a simple schematic that shows you all the sources of solid
waste. Whatever we do in our daily lives, whether it is a process or the use of a product,
we are manufacturing these products using certain raw materials, and in the process of
extracting those raw materials, processing them, using them to manufacture different
products, waste is generated. So solid waste is coming out of each step in the process and
manufacturing is not just primary manufacturing. We have secondary manufacturing and
higher levels. The products then go to the consumer, the consumer may use it and then
dispose it of or they may reuse or recycle it. So, you have processing and recovery where
it may be recycled, which means manufacturing a new product out of the old waste, and
if that is not possible, then it is finally disposed off. So, we will be spending a lot of time
on each of these points in the remaining part of this week. So, what you see here is the
materials flow and the generation of waste in any technological society.
Let us now take a look at the principles of integrated solid waste management. So, we
will start with the waste management hierarchy, which you are probably familiar with,
and the same goes for the four Rs. We will look at some green design principles and
policy issues.

So, let me start with the waste management hierarchy. This is a triangle that most of you
may be familiar with. The greenest option is at the top, and the least green option is at the
bottom. So, our first priority is to prevent the generation of waste. We want to ensure that
waste is not generated. How can we do that? By reducing the amount of resources
consumed. So, source reduction or prevention of waste generation is our first priority.

And the second one is waste minimization. So, once you know that okay, you have to
have a product or a process and some amount of waste has to be generated, you want to
minimize that waste. We will go to each one of these points in the subsequent slides.

Now, what is reuse? Reuse means that you do not modify anything. For example the
simplest example is this water bottle. I bought this water bottle and it was a brand new
bottle, but I am reusing it. I use the same water bottle a few dozen times. Okay, so that is
a simple example of reuse. I am not modifying the product. I am not throwing it out. I am
using it as far as I can. Now, supposing this water bottle gets damaged. It is no longer
usable. I then put it in a waste bin, and the recycling person, the recyclist if I may use the
word, they will take it to a recycling facility. This plastic bottle or PET bottle will be
melted down. It may be converted into different forms of plastic. It will be used to
manufacture a new product, and many of the buckets and household plastic items that
you see have a, they have the recycled symbol on them and that symbol shows that it is
recycled plastic. So, that is called recycling where you are remanufacturing a product out
of the waste material.

Now, you have to keep in mind that as we go down this triangle, there is an input of
additional energy at every step. Prevention does not require any additional energy. Waste
minimization does not require additional energy. Reuse is minimal amount of energy. In
the example that I just gave you, there is no additional energy. I am simply using the
bottle again and again and again. However, when we go from reuse to recycling, there is
a significant amount of new energy required for creating a new product.

Now, supposing you have material that cannot be recycled and instead, you want to get
some energy out of it, and we will be looking at those options again later. Now, you can
convert waste to energy after processing it. So, you have to segregate it. You have to
make sure that it is in a particular form so that you can actually capture the heat energy
that is part of the waste. Now, that itself is a somewhat complicated process and the final
process is treatment and disposal. So, whether you treat the waste, whether you put it in a
landfill or you take it to an incinerator and convert it to energy. So, these two options,
waste to energy, treatment, and disposal, sometimes they are done together in a materials
recovery facility, sometimes they are done in separate facilities. It depends on the nature
of the municipal facilities that are created. So, we may have time to look at some of these
maybe.
Let us go to the 4 Rs. What are the 4 Rs? The 4 Rs are reduce, reuse, recycle, and
recover. We want to reduce. The first principle is reduce the amount of material used. We
want to remember source reduction. This is source reduction. So, we want to reduce the
amount of material required to deliver one unit of product without sacrificing utility or
quality. You can use refills instead of new containers. For example, this water bottle, I
am refilling it with water instead of buying a new bottle. So that is one example. There
are several other examples in the market. You can use spray painting versus brush or roll-
up painting. You can use rechargeable batteries versus disposable batteries. You can
increase the lifetime of the product. There are certain materials, for example, synthetic
fabrics have a longer life than natural fabric. So, under certain conditions, you may want
to use those fabrics instead of natural fabrics if they last longer. Similarly, when you have
a choice, when you have to choose between plastic versus cheap wood furniture. So,
some people would argue that why use wood furniture when you can use plastic and it
lasts longer. So those kinds of arguments and debates are going on in society, and they
will continue for some time. Eliminate or reduce the need for a certain product.

We have moved to electronic media in a big way. I remember that 30 years ago, almost
everything was in print form. So, your books, your newspapers, your magazines,
everything was in print form. Today, we are moving to electronic form. Today we are
delivering information in an electronic format, not in a print format. Most of us prefer the
electronic format. It has greater flexibility and less resource consumption. So, it is one of
the best examples of source reduction. Similarly, you can use gas pipelines to deliver
kitchen gas to the homes of consumers instead of using gas cylinders. You save on
transportation, you save on certain hazard potential due to transportation, and so on. So,
these are examples of source reduction in terms of either material or energy usage. I have
put in a little note at the bottom that sometimes use of disposables is necessary for quality
and convenience. And the COVID lockdown has really brought this home to all of us that
under serious, when there are serious medical problems, it is necessary to use disposables
to ensure that health and convenience are maintained. But, there are consequences in
terms of an increase in resource consumption and waste generation. So, this kind of
waste, the biomedical part of the solid waste world is going to require special attention
and we do have regulations for these types of materials. So that is a different topic. I am
not going to go any further, but just to say that sometimes it is necessary to use
disposables. You cannot avoid it.
The next point is reuse. Most of us are using many of the products that we buy are reused
in our homes. So, plastic materials, metals, glass containers, most of us use it for storage
for other purposes than the original purposes. So that is very common. I have also
mentioned in a somewhat humorous note over here that it is a sad fact that in India, the
street vendors use paper, printed paper, newsprint, magazine paper for serving food. That
is definitely not healthy, but it is an example of reuse. Our traditional method of using
leaf dishes for serving food was better and a healthier disposable. So, whether we are
going in the right direction or not is for all of us to examine.

So, recycling requires segregation of the individual components of the waste into separate
categories. You need to separate the plastics from paper from different types of metal,
whether they are steel or aluminum cans, whether they are glass materials, your yard
waste has to be separated from all of this. If you have electronic waste, we will be
spending one lecture on that, and each one of these waste streams has to be separated for
recycling. Not everyone in the country is doing that. Most cities and towns still do not
have segregation at source. Now, where the refuse is not segregated, the desired materials
have to be separated using a combination of manual and mechanical means at a central
facility. So we call this a materials recovery facility where the mixed waste is brought
into that facility and we can do either manual or mechanical segregation to take it for
recycling. So first we have to recover it in a central facility and then take it for recycling.

Then, we come to extended product life. One of the other source reduction principles is to
increase the life of the product. What you notice in today's age is that the lifetime of the
products we use is decreasing. Consumer goods are the best example of that. Our
computers, clothing, electrical and electronic appliances, even our motor vehicles, their
lifetimes are becoming shorter and shorter. Now, if the lifetime of the product is shorter,
it means you are using more resources, not less. What is the benefit of extending the
product life? Resource and energy consumption can be reduced. If you have a longer
product life, and the overall environmental impacts can also be reduced. There are several
examples of that. Electrical and electronic goods are the best examples. Electrical pens,
batteries, cartridges, these are common consumer goods. All of them, refills are available
but most of the manufacturers discourage use of these refills because of another problem
and that is the profit margin. I would not go too far with that. I will move to the next
point and that is material life extension. We should be using products which can be
recycled easily. So, one issue that would help to reduce the amount of waste generated is
that you buy products which can be recycled easily. So, the best examples are paper and
plastics and the reuse of these materials in the product itself. To put it another way, you
buy products that may have paper, plastic, and so on, but they should be in a form that
can be recycled easily.

Selection of materials should be based not just on cost criteria. 30 to 40 years ago, cost
was considered the only criteria for choosing a material, product or process. But, if we
change our mindset and say that we are not going to choose only on the basis of cost but
also on the basis of human health and ecological impacts, would that be better for the
environment and the answer is obviously yes. So here at the bottom, I have 5 toxic heavy
metals that are listed. Arsenic, Chromium, Cadmium, Mercury, and Lead. These 5 heavy
metals are known to have zero beneficial value as nutrients. All the other metals have
some beneficial value as nutrients at very low levels, many of them at very low levels.
Now, these 5, if they are present in any product or process have to be eliminated. As I
said, people are using alloys and plastics instead of metals. One example of that even
though cast iron, CI stands for cast iron, cast iron used to be used for supplying water to
homes and offices and so on, that has been substituted now by plastic pipes, and that has
been a successful substitution for the simple reason that corrosion of cast iron pipes, their
longevity and so on has made plastic more popular. So, this is one example of
substitution. Now, our packaging materials were often made of plastics. So, you have
thermocol, and you have several other grades of plastic that were used in packaging
materials. They are being substituted by biodegradable plastics. We have never used lead
in the solder that we use in India, but in other countries people were using lead both in
paints as well as in soldering, and that has also been eliminated. It has been eliminated
from paint, it has been eliminated from petroleum, it has been eliminated from solder.
Communication, we have been using the first generation of communication cables were

communication because of again this big improvement because you are using less
resources to deliver the same product or rather the same idea in this case. You can reduce
or replace the use of toxic materials. We are moving away from the use of fluorescent
