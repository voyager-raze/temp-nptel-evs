When discussing ocean tides, the focus is on harvesting the potential energy within the

Another source of energy is the salinity gradient, which occurs where freshwater from a

Ocean energy offers several key advantages over other renewable sources like solar, wind,
or geothermal energy, primarily due to its vast theoretical resource potential, which could
meet both current and future global electricity demands. According to the International
Renewable Energy Agency (IRENA), the combined potential of all ocean energy
technologies ranges between 45,000 terawatt-hours to well over 1,300,000 terawatt-hours
of electricity per year, enough to cover more than twice the current global demand.
Additionally, ocean energy is more predictable than other variable renewable resources,
such as solar photovoltaics and wind energy, due to the consistent influence of the moon's
movement. This predictability provides a technical edge and allows for continuous
electricity generation, especially when coupled with offshore wind energy, as waves persist
even after wind speeds decrease. The socio-economic benefits of ocean energy are also
significant, including job creation in research, development, deployment, and the operation
and maintenance of ocean energy power plants. Unlike wind energy, ocean energy
technologies are submerged and do not disrupt the natural landscape, leading to increased
social acceptance. Furthermore, as a renewable resource, ocean energy helps reduce
greenhouse gas emissions from conventional electricity generation, contributing to climate
change mitigation. It also addresses specific needs of small island developing states, such
as providing reliable electricity, sustainable transportation, and freshwater through
desalination. Due to these numerous benefits, ocean energy is gaining popularity alongside
wind and geothermal energy.
Tides occur as a result of the interaction between the gravitational forces of the moon and
the sun, leading to a natural rise and fall in seawater levels, which we refer to as tides.
These tidal movements can be harnessed to generate electricity. Tidal energy can be
exploited by capturing the potential energy created by the difference in water levels or by
utilizing the kinetic energy in the flow of incoming and outgoing water, particularly when
the flow speed is at least 1.5 to 2 meters per second.
Although the typical sea level difference between high and low tide is usually below 1
meter, the tidal range can exceed 20 meters in some locations. The potential for tidal energy
increases with the tidal range, but it can only be harnessed in a limited number of countries
with the necessary geographic conditions. Due to this geographic limitation, the theoretical
potential of tidal energy is the smallest among ocean energy technologies, estimated at
1,200 terawatt-hours per year. While tidal energy has a much lower potential compared to
other renewable resources like solar radiation or wind, its main advantage is that tides are
predictable and not influenced by weather, but rather by cyclical constellations.

Tidal energy technologies can be broadly categorized into two groups: tidal barrage and
tidal stream technologies. Tidal barrage technologies harness the potential energy from the
tidal range, which is the difference in height between high and low tides. On the other hand,
tidal stream technologies utilize the kinetic energy from the direct flow of incoming and
outgoing tides in open water. Tidal barrage systems are relatively more mature compared
to other ocean energy technologies, with power plants in operation since the 1960s. The
development of tidal range hydropower is often associated with estuarine developments.
Tidal stream technologies, on the other hand, harness the kinetic energy from the direct
flow of incoming and outgoing tides in open water. While tidal barrage systems utilize the
tidal range to capture potential energy, tidal stream technologies focus on the movement of
water itself. Tidal barrage technology is more mature compared to other ocean energy
technologies, with power plants having been in operation since the 1960s. The
development of tidal range hydropower is often linked to estuarine developments, where
the tidal range is significant enough to generate power efficiently.

As illustrated in the figure, tidal barrage development typically involves estuarine areas
where a barrage is constructed to enclose an estuary, creating a single reservoir behind it.
This reservoir is crucial to the operation of the system and incorporates conventional low-
head hydro turbines. The process works by filling the basin with water during high tides.
The sluice gates, represented by the ash-colored bars in the figure, manage the flow of
water into the basin, which is then released through the turbines to generate electricity.

The ash-colored bars represent the sluice gates. These gates are shut when the basin is filled
with water during high tide. Then, during low tide, the gates are opened, allowing water to
flow from the tidal basin through the turbines inside the barrage, generating electricity.
After several hours, the gates close again, and the process repeats.

Tidal stream technologies, unlike tidal barrages, directly utilize the incoming and outgoing
flow in open water to generate electricity. Tidal streams are currents in the sea that flow as
tides move in and out. Tidal stream turbines, which resemble wind turbines, use fast-
moving tidal streams instead of wind to generate electricity. These turbines are approaching
maturity and are expected to become more prominent than tidal barrages in the near future.

Tidal stream turbines come in various designs. For example, horizontal-axis turbines have
blades radially attached to a horizontal shaft. As the tidal current flows past the blades, it
causes rotation, generating power much like a wind turbine, but underwater. Vertical-axis
turbines have blades parallel to a rotating shaft, allowing them to generate power regardless
of the direction of the tidal flow.

Another design, known as the enclosed tips or open center turbine, increases the velocity
of tidal streams by concentrating the flow in a funnel or duct where a turbine is placed to
generate electricity. Reciprocating devices or oscillating hydrofoils work by lifting an
oscillating hydrofoil attached to an arm with the tidal flow. The up-and-down movement
of the foil drives a shaft or pistons to generate electricity.
The Archimedes screw or spiral design uses a helical-shaped impeller that turns when the
tidal stream passes through it, converting the rotation into electricity. Tidal kites, which are
connected to the seabed or a floating platform, move through the tidal stream in a figure-
eight or linear trajectory, increasing the relative velocity and electricity output.
In addition to these seven technologies, other designs have been investigated, though they
do not exclusively fit into the category of tidal technology. Now, let's consider how much
power can be generated from a tidal barrage. In this setup, water enters an enclosed tidal
basin at high tide and is released at low tide, generating electricity as it passes through
turbines.

To understand the potential energy available from a tidal barrage, consider a rectangular
tidal basin with an area A. The total mass of water in the tidal basin above the low tide
level is given by m=AH, where  is the density of water, and H is the tidal range, or the
difference in height between high tide and low tide. This total mass is calculated by
multiplying the water density , the area of the tidal basin A, and the tidal range H. The
centre of gravity of this water mass is located at the height of ½H above the low tide level.
Therefore, the maximum potential energy available per tide, assuming all the waterfalls
through this height, is given by ½gAH2, where g is the acceleration due to gravity. The
average power generated by the tidal barrage can be calculated by dividing this potential
energy by the tidal period T, resulting in the expression (gAH2)/2T, which represents the
average potential power that can be extracted from the tidal barrage.
Wave energy converters are devices designed to harvest the energy contained in ocean
waves and convert it into electricity. When wind blows over the ocean, it transmits some
of its kinetic energy to the ocean's surface, creating wave energy, which comprises both
kinetic energy and gravitational potential energy. Wave energy converters can be designed
to absorb kinetic energy, typically through moving bodies, or potential energy, often
through overtopping devices or attenuators, or both, as in the case of point absorbers. These
devices are specifically designed to capture either kinetic energy, potential energy, or a
combination of both.

The highest part of a wave is called the crest, while the lowest part is known as the trough.
The vertical distance between the crest and the trough is referred to as the wave height. The
distance between two successive crests or two successive troughs is called the wavelength.
The amplitude of a wave is the distance from the crest to the flat water level of the sea, and
it is essentially half the wave height. The time it takes for successive crests or troughs to
pass a fixed point is known as the period T. Another important concept is group velocity,
which is the speed at which a wave packet travels. A wave packet occurs when two or more
waves exist simultaneously, which is typically the case in the ocean.
Wave height is influenced by several factors, including wind speed, wind duration (how
long the wind is blowing), and the fetch, which is the distance over water that the wind
blows in a single direction. If the wind speed is low, only small waves will form. If the
wind speed is high but lasts only for a few minutes, large waves will not develop. Similarly,
if strong winds blow for a long time but over a short fetch, large waves will not form. Large
waves only occur when wind speed, wind duration, and fetch all align favorably.
The global theoretical potential of wave energy is approximately 29,500 terawatt-hours per
year, meaning that wave energy alone could meet the entire global energy demand. Despite
seasonal variations and short-term fluctuations, wave energy can be reliably forecasted,
making it a widely considered dependable energy resource. The global distribution of wave
power levels, as shown in the accompanying figure, indicates that mid-latitude regions
have lower resource levels, while latitudes between 30 and 60 degrees, particularly in the
southern hemisphere, experience more powerful waves.
Unlike tidal energy, wave energy technologies have not yet converged on a single design
approach, similar to what we've seen in wind energy. Over the years, three main working
principles have emerged for harnessing energy from waves: oscillating water columns,
oscillating bodies, and overtopping devices. Oscillating water columns use wave motion to
compress air and drive a turbine, generating electricity. Oscillating bodies transform the
motion between bodies caused by waves into electricity, while overtopping devices capture
the potential energy of water that spills into a closed reservoir, subsequently driving a
hydraulic turbine.

Oscillating water column systems work by inducing varying pressure levels between an
air-filled chamber and the atmosphere, with high-velocity air exhausting through a turbine
coupled to an electrical generator as waves rise, and reversing airflow when waves fall,
generating continuous electricity. Overtopping devices capture passing waves in a
reservoir, releasing water through a shaft where a turbine generates energy. Attenuators
consist of connected segments or a flexible part that extracts energy by following the
parallel motion of waves, while point absorbers use a buoy that moves with the waves to
generate energy. Oscillating water surge columns capture the back-and-forth motion of
waves in an oscillating arm, and submerged pressure differential systems use the pressure
changes caused by passing waves to trigger pumps that generate electricity.

Additional technologies include bulge wave devices, which capture energy from wave
surges through a flexible device with water passing through a turbine, and rotating mass
systems, where the heaving and swaying of waves cause a weight to rotate within the
device, driving a generator. Oscillating water column technology was one of the earliest
wave energy technologies researched, and while it remains in use, fewer companies are
currently focusing on it. In the oscillating body category, 5 projects and 14 devices are
operational globally, with point absorbers being the most widely tested and deployed due
to their scalability.

Overtopping devices have only been tested in subscale models and are not widely pursued.
In summary, tidal stream and wave energy technologies are rapidly advancing, with
numerous projects being scaled up to units of 1 megawatt or higher worldwide. Tidal
stream technology is more advanced and nearing commercialization, with several tidal
arrays in development, while wave energy, though advancing quickly, remains less mature
and is primarily in the prototype and demonstration phase.
