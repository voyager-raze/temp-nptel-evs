Much of the heat within the Earth is radiogenic, i.e., from the radioactive decay of certain elements

Geothermal energy currently exploited occur in a number of geological environments. However,
the vast majority of medium- and high temperature geothermal systems, which are suitable for

The total thermal energy contained in the Earth is of the order of 12.6 x 1012 EJ and that of the
crust of the order of 5.4 x 109 EJ to depths of up to 50 km. In practice geothermal plants can only
utilize a portion of the stored thermal energy due to limitations in drilling technology and rock
permeability.
Geothermal resources available for utilization can be classified into three broad categories:
convective or hydrothermal systems, conductive systems, and deep aquifers. Convective or
hydrothermal systems include both liquid and vapor-dominated types, with temperatures ranging
from 100°C to over 300°C. Conductive systems typically involve rock and magma across a wide
range of temperatures. Deep aquifer systems contain circulating fluids in porous media or fracture
zones at depths greater than 3 kilometers, but unlike the other two, they lack a localized magmatic
heat source.
When comparing the global distribution of geothermal resources, it is important to note that high-
temperature convective hydrothermal resources are most commonly found near tectonic plate
boundaries, often associated with volcanoes and seismic activity. This is because the Earth's crust
in these regions is highly fractured and permeable to fluids, making heat sources readily accessible.

Geothermal systems are made up of three main components: a heat source, a heat sink and a heat
exchanger. Typically, the heat source is the ground while the heat sink is a built environment (in
general, a structure). However, the opposite can also occur, that is the heat source is a built
environment while the heat sink is the ground. The heat exchanger is generally constituted by one
or more elements containing a fluid that transfers the heat between the heat source and the heat
sink. There are three main types of geothermal energy systems:
Geothermal power plants to generate electricity
Direct use and district heating systems involves directly using heat from hot water within the earth
Geothermal heat pumps use the Earth's shallow ground temperature for heating and cooling

The first type of geothermal power plant is the dry steam power plant. In these plants, a steam
turbine is used to directly convert low-pressure, high-volume steam from the steam field into
electricity. Dry steam plants typically use condensing turbines, with the condensate either re-
injected into the ground or evaporated in wet cooling towers. This type of plant requires steam at
150°C or higher, and the steam entering the turbine must be at least 99.999% dry to prevent scaling
or erosion of turbine and piping components. The term "dry steam" comes from this requirement
for extremely dry steam. These plants range in size from 8 megawatts to 140 megawatts.
Next, we have flash steam power plants, the most common type of geothermal electricity plants
today. These are similar to dry steam plants but use a separation process called flashing to obtain
steam. The steam is then directed to turbines, and the resulting condensate is either re-injected or
further flashed at lower pressure. Flash steam plants operate best with well temperatures above
180°C. The exiting fluid and steam condensate are usually re-injected. Flash plants vary in size:
single flash plants range from 0.2 to 0.8 megawatts, double flash plants from 2 to 110 megawatts,
and triple flash plants from 60 to 150 megawatts.
In addition to the previous two configurations, there are binary power plants. These are typically
used in low and intermediate-enthalpy geothermal fields, where the geothermal fluid heats a
secondary working fluid in a closed loop via heat exchangers. Unlike dry steam and flash steam
plants, the geothermal fluid in binary power plants doesn't directly drive the turbines. Instead, it
transfers heat to a working fluid, such as an ammonia-water mixture or a hydrocarbon, chosen for
its boiling and condensation points that better match the geothermal resource temperature. Binary
power plants are generally used for resource temperatures between 100°C and 170°C
.
In locations where natural geothermal resources like steam or hot water do not exist, the heat from
the rock can still be harnessed by creating artificial permeability for fluids to extract that heat.
These systems are known as Enhanced Geothermal Systems (EGS) or Engineered Geothermal
Systems. EGS enables the use of low permeability, low porosity conductive systems, low
productivity convective systems, and deep aquifers by creating fluid connectivity through
hydraulic stimulation and advanced well configurations. EGS requires simulation of subsurface
regions where temperatures are sufficiently high for effective energy utilization.
Direct use geothermal systems use groundwater that is heated by natural geological processes
below the Earth's surface. This water can be as hot as 200°F or more. Bodies of hot groundwater
can be found in many areas with volcanic or tectonic activity. In locations such as Yellowstone
National Park and Iceland, these groundwater reservoirs can reach the surface, creating geysers
and hot springs. One can pump hot water from the surface or from underground for a wide range
of useful applications, including large-scale pool heating; space heating, cooling, and on-demand
hot water for buildings of most sizes; district heating (i.e., heat for multiple buildings in a city);
heating roads and sidewalks to melt snow; and some industrial and agricultural processes.

Pumping: To tap into hot ground water, a well is drilled. A pumping system may be installed,
although in some cases, hot water or steam may rise up through the well without active pumping.

Delivery: Hot water or steam can be used directly in a variety of applications, or it can be cycled
through a heat exchanger.

Refilling: Depending on the use requirements of the system and the conditions of the site, the
ground water aquifer may need to be replenished with water from the surface. In some cases, the
movement of ground water might refill the aquifer naturally.
This system is commonly used because of the chemical composition of the geothermal water. In
both cases the spent geothermal water is disposed of into injection wells and a conventional backup
boiler may be provided to meet peak demand.

Next, we have geothermal heat pumps (GHPs). These systems utilize the constant underground
temperature of the shallow earth as thermal storage, enabling efficient heating and cooling. Like
other heat pumps, GHPs move heat from one place to another using electricity, similar to how air
conditioners and refrigerators operate. Geothermal heat pumps can be used to heat and cool
buildings by taking advantage of the relatively constant temperatures found about 30 feet below
the Earth's surface, which typically range between 10°C and 15°C year-round.
Now, let's consider the environmental and social aspects of geothermal energy. Generally, the
negative environmental impacts associated with geothermal energy are minor. The production of
hot fluids can emit small quantities of greenhouse gases, primarily naturally sourced CO, that
would eventually be released into the atmosphere through natural venting. Importantly, geothermal
energy does not generate additional CO since it involves no combustion process.
Water is not a limiting factor for geothermal power generation because geothermal fluids are
typically brines. Flash power plants do not consume potable water for cooling and produce
condensed water that, with proper treatment, can be used for agricultural and industrial purposes.
Binary power plants can further minimize water use by employing air cooling. Potential adverse
effects, such as the disposal of geothermal fluids, induced seismicity, and ground subsidence, can
be mitigated through sound practices. These practices can also optimize water and land use,
enhance long-term sustainability, and protect natural thermal features valued by the community.
However, the success of geothermal projects often hinges on the acceptance of local communities.
To secure social acceptance, it is crucial to prevent or minimize detrimental environmental
impacts, protect land occupiers, and create tangible benefits for local residents. Public education
and awareness of potential impacts are also essential. The prerequisites for gaining local support
include: first, preventing adverse effects on people's health; second, minimizing environmental
impacts; and third, creating direct and ongoing benefits for the community.

Geothermal development creates local job opportunities during exploration, drilling, and
construction phases, as well as permanent full-time jobs when power plants start operating. This
is because geothermal fields, where fluids are extracted, must be operated locally. Such
development can alleviate rural poverty in many developing countries in Asia, Central and South
America, and Africa, where geothermal resources are often located in remote mountainous areas.
Some geothermal companies and government agencies have also addressed social issues by
improving local security, building roads, schools, medical facilities, and other community assets,
funded by profits from operating power plants.

Geothermal energy currently provides electricity in more than 30 countries across the globe, with
installed capacity per country ranging from less than 1 megawatt to 3.7 gigawatts. The main
technologies employed for electricity generation include dry steam, flash steam, and binary power
plants. The regions with the largest installed capacity are Asia, Oceania, North America, and
Eurasia. The use of geothermal energy for electricity generation is more advanced in some regions
and countries than in others, and this development is not always directly correlated with the
presence of suitable geothermal resources. For instance, even in favourable volcanic settings like
the Asia-Pacific Ring of Fire, geothermal development shows significant differences across
regions and countries. North America and Central America have more mature geothermal
industries compared to South America and the Caribbean islands. The global electricity generation
capacity of geothermal plants has grown from 200 megawatts in the early 1950s to approximately
16 gigawatts in 2020. This rise is driven not only by the Paris Agreement and the need to
decarbonize energy infrastructure but also by the fact that geothermal energy, being locally
available, allows nations to reduce their dependence on imported fossil fuels for electricity
generation. Since 2000, installed geothermal capacity has increased at an average annual rate of
about 3%, with significant contributions from Indonesia, Kenya, Turkey, and the United States.
Despite this growth, geothermal energy represents only 0.5% of the global renewable electricity
market as of 2022.
The global installed capacity for direct energy use of geothermal energy for heating and cooling
was 117 gigawatts in 2020. Geothermal heat pumps (GHPs) constitute 72% of this capacity, with
the remaining 28% coming from direct heating and cooling using geothermal fluids. Geothermal
heating and cooling applications are concentrated in three regions: Asia, Oceania, and Eurasia,
with a combined installed capacity of 45.8 gigawatts, followed by North America. Latin America,
the Caribbean, Africa, and the Middle East together contribute less than 1% to the global capacity.
The geothermal heating and cooling industry experienced a 52% growth in installed thermal
capacity and a 74% growth in utilization between 2015 and 2020. Over the same period, the
installed capacity in the GHP sector grew by 54%, and utilization increased by 84%. The largest
geothermal heating and cooling applications worldwide are GHPs, space heating (both individual
and district), and agri-food applications. In the last 10 years, geothermal heating and cooling have
also expanded into various sectors, including fish farming and snow melting.
Many countries, both in developing and developed regions, are making strides in deploying more
geothermal heating and cooling projects for agri-food and industrial applications. Climate policies
are expected to be one of the main driving factors for future geothermal deployment. It is projected
that by 2050, the installed capacity of geothermal power plants will be between 140 gigawatts and
160 gigawatts, with half of this capacity being in Enhanced Geothermal Systems (EGS). The
potential installed capacity for direct uses could reach as high as 800 gigawatts.
In summary, there is a tremendous amount of energy in the form of heat available below the
ground, which, if utilized effectively, can be used not only for electricity generation but also for
direct heating, cooling, and several other applications. As climate actions intensify and the need to
