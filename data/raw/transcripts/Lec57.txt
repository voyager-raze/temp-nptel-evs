Let us start with one fundamental concept. Very often you will find that in
environmental legislation you use we have the word act versus rule. So, what is the
difference between an act versus a rule? An act is a law. Rules are the procedure that
is required to follow that law. So, we will be looking at certain acts and the rules that
come out of those acts. So, an act also means what should be done. It provides a basic
framework for the law. Rules are guidelines. They can be modified. The act is more
difficult to modify. The act contains substantive provisions and the rules specify the
procedure for implementing the provisions of the act. The act is the statute which
prescribes the action in terms of its provisions and the rules come from the act that is
part of the statute to enable the provisions to be implemented. The act contains main
legal provisions on a particular subject. Rules are mainly procedural. Passing an act
through parliament is a very time-consuming exercise. Making rules is fairly easy.
They have been amended several times and we will be looking at that briefly in the
next few slides.
So, these are the laws that we are going to look at, the laws as well as the rules that
come from the acts. We are going to be spending some time in this lecture on each
one of them and they are arranged here in chronological order. So, we will start with
the Water Prevention and Control of Pollution Act, Air Prevention and Control of
Pollution Act, Environmental Protection Act. These are the three acts. All other
regulations are basically rules. We have the Hazardous Waste Management Handling
and Transboundary Movement Rules, Biomedical Waste Handling Rules, Flyash
Rules, Municipal Solid Waste Management and Handling Rules, Battery Management
and Handling Rules, Plastic Waste Management and Handling Rules, E-Waste
Management and Handling Rules and then Construction and Demolition Waste
Management Rules.
So let us start with the first one. What is the Water Act? The Water Act was passed in
1974. It was in response to India signing the decision taken at the UN Conference on
Human Environment in Stockholm in 1972. So, this act was enacted in India in 1974
and it was last amended. It has gone through several amendments. It was last
amended in 2003. The main objective of the Water Act was to provide for prevention,
control and abatement of water pollution and maintenance and restoration of the
water quality. One of the biggest points about this act was that central and state
pollution control boards were established in the country to enforce these regulations,
to collect and analyse samples, to impose penalties. If needed, industrial units could
be closed by the Pollution Control Boards and they could publish the names of the
offending industrial units. This particular act applies to all wastewater discharges,
both municipal and industrial.
We then come to the Air Prevention and Control of Pollution Act in 1981. The main
objectives of the act were to prevent, control and abate air pollution. Again, the
powers of the central and state pollution boards were increased to allow them to take
samples, air quality samples. Remember in the first act, in the Water Act, it was only
water samples. This act helped to broaden the scope of activities of CPCB and the
SPCBs to take air samples as well and to ensure that they met air quality standards,
discharge standards as well as ambient standards. The Air Prevention and Control of
Pollution Act was enacted in 1981. It was amended in 1987. According to this act, no
person can operate industries that include asbestos, cement, fertilizer and petroleum
without consent of the state board. In fact, I think this list is much longer now. It also
states that sources of air pollution such as industry vehicles, power plants are not
permitted to release particulate matter, lead, carbon monoxide, sulphur dioxide,
nitrogen oxide, volatile organic compounds and other toxic substances beyond a
prescribed level. So, the discharge standards, the emission standards are decided by
CPCB. The actual values and the parameters are given on the internet website. You
can refer to them and all this is mainly under the Water Act and the Air Act. So, these
two acts were designed to provide CPCB and SPCB the powers to implement the
provisions of the two acts and to take samples and to determine who the offenders
are.
It was soon realized that simply having an Air Act and a Water Act is not sufficient.
You need something more holistic that will take care of every part of the environment
and not just air and water. And there were several other issues that came into the
limelight and because of that, the Environment Protection Act was enacted in 1986
with the objective of providing protection and improvement of the environment. The
act was last amended in 1991. It allows the central government to establish
authorities with the mandate to prevent environmental pollution in all its forms and
to tackle environmental problems that are peculiar to different parts of the country.
According to this act, environment word includes air, water, land and the internal
relationship between all of these and human beings, other living creatures, plants,
microorganisms and property. So, you can see that it has an overriding scope as
against Air and Water Act which have a limited scope. As part of the Environment
Protection Act, it became mandatory to have environmental impact assessments for
all large projects that are proposed by any agency within the country. In the last
lecture in this series, I will be talking about one case study based on the
environmental impact assessment.
So, we come to the first set of rules after the Environmental Protection Act was
passed. These are the Hazardous Waste Management, Handling and Transboundary
Movement Rules. They were first passed in 1989. There have been several
amendments since then in 2000, in 2003 and perhaps in between and the latest
amendment is in 2022. The current notifications supersede all former notifications.
What is hazardous waste? According to the rules, hazardous waste implies that any
waste because of its physical, chemical, reactive, toxic, flammable, explosive or
corrosive characteristics causes danger or is likely to cause danger to the health of
human beings or to the health of human beings and other organisms or to the
environment either by itself or when it is in contact with other hazardous waste
materials or substances. So, you can see some of the symbols that are used on many
chemicals and compounds and containers. You will often find these kinds of symbols
to make everyone aware of the fact that these are hazardous materials. The
Hazardous Waste Management Rules were notified to ensure safe handling,
generation, processing, treatment, packaging, storage, transportation, use,
reprocessing, collection, conversion and finally offering this waste for sale,
destruction and disposal. So one of the most important things that has happened after
the passage of these rules is that we now have treatment, storage and disposal
facilities for hazardous waste in different states. So, these TSDF facilities have been
created in many different locations. The rules are also important because they define
the duties of various authorities including the Ministry of Environment, Forests and
Climate Change, the Central Pollution Control Board, the State and Union Territory
governments, the State Pollution Control Boards, the Foreign Trade, DGFT stands for
the Directorate General of Foreign Trade, Port Authorities, Custom Authorities all of
them have been given clear responsibilities and duties wherever hazardous waste
generation, handling and disposal is part of their work. Another important point in
the latest notification is that waste rubber tires have now been included in this and
the extended producer responsibility which started with e-waste, I will be talking
about that later, but something that came into effect in the rules with e-waste is now
part of hazardous waste, it is part of batteries waste or rather waste batteries as well
as e-waste and this is as of 2022.

So here we have the Biomedical Waste Handling Rules, these rules were notified in
1998 by the Ministry of Environment and Forests as part of the Environmental
Protection Act. These rules apply to all persons who generate, collect, receive, store,
transport, treat, dispose or handle biomedical waste in any form. Keep in mind that
biomedical waste comes from several different sources. So, you have hospitals,
nursing homes, laboratories, pathology labs, any other possible source of research
labs all of them can be the generators of biomedical waste. So, any of these sources
are under the purview of this particular set of rules. The prescribed authority for
enforcement of these rules in respect of all healthcare facilities located in any state or
union territory is under the State Pollution Control Board. They were recently revised
in March of 2016.
There are several biomedical waste categories. There are four major categories that
are shown in this slide and the next. So, the yellow category includes human, animal
and anatomical waste, waste contaminated with blood or body fluids, expired or
discarded medicines, microbiological, biotechnical and clinical lab waste and
discarded disinfectants. That is in the yellow category. And remember these three
intersecting circles are also the symbol that is used for biomedical waste. The color
code red is for contaminated waste. So, waste from disposable items, tubings, bottles,
intravenous tubes, catheters, urine bags, syringes, all these come under the red
category.
The blue category is for sharps, anything that is sharp material, metallic sharp
material. So, needles, syringes, needle cutters, scalpels, blades, contaminated sharp
objects that can cause cuts and punctures and so on.
So they all come in the blue category. And the white category is for glassware, broken,
discarded or contaminated glass, including medicine vials, ampoules, anything
contaminated with cytotoxic waste and metallic body implants. All of that comes in
the white category.

Then we have the fly ash rules in 1999. Now it is very important for everyone to
understand where this fly ash is coming from. So here you see a photo of a thermal
power plant. In a thermal power plant, there are two parts. There is a furnace inside
where the coal is burnt for generating electricity. There will be some amount of ash
that will fall to the bottom of the furnace. And as the name suggests, it is called bottom
ash. There is another part to the thermal power plant and that is the flue gases. So,
you have your chimney stacks. These are the smoke stacks or the chimneys through
which the hot gases will rise and go into the atmosphere. Now they may pass through
air pollution control equipment. In any case, whether the air pollution control
equipment is there or not, you will get very fine particulate matter will also go along
with the hot gases into the atmosphere. So if there is air pollution control equipment,
it will capture the fine particulate matter. And if there is no equipment, it will go into
the atmosphere. Now assuming that there is sufficient capture of this fine particulate
matter by the air pollution control equipment, that fine particulate matter is called fly
ash. Fly for the simple reason that is flying out of the chimney or the smoke stack. And
this material has cementaceous or pozzolanic properties. So, it was found that one of
the best reuse and recycling applications of fly ash is to use it for making bricks. So,
the central government issued directions for restricting the excavation of topsoil for
manufacture of bricks, promoting the utilization of fly ash in the manufacture of
building materials and in construction activities within a radius of 100 km from a coal
or lignite based thermal power plant. The amendment notification came on 3rd
November 2009. It set a timeline for achieving the target of 100% utilization of fly
ash. It was observed that the thermal power plants have not yet achieved 100%
utilization. In the next slide, you will see some statistics regarding this and they have
achieved about 92% in 2020-21. It has also been observed that construction agencies
are not using fly ash and fly ash-based products in their construction projects. There
is an urgent need to provide additional measures for utilization of fly ash and fly ash-
based products in the country. There is also some fear of the presence of toxic heavy
metals in the fly ash. Remember that when coal is being burnt, all of the combustible
material will be burnt. But what is left behind in the ash is heavy metal content
including certain radioactive elements and so on. And therefore, there is an
enrichment in the ash of toxic heavy metals as well as radioactive elements. How
much enrichment there is and whether it is significant enough to cause health
problems, these questions are very important and they need to be answered. And
another point, keeping these questions in mind and clear answers to these questions,
it is important to understand that fly ash-based products are best used in outdoor
environments and not in indoor environments. Recently, fly ash management and
utilization mission was constituted by the National Green Tribunal. The aim of the
mission was to coordinate and monitor issues relating to the handling and disposal
of fly ash. At this point, what is the situation in terms of utilization of fly ash? As of
2020-21, number of thermal power plants from which the central electrical authority
was able to get data was 202 thermal power plants, there are perhaps 1000s in the
country. The installed capacity in terms of megawatts was approximately 2 lakh
megawatts or 2.1 lakh megawatts. The coal consumed in terms of millions of tons was
686. The percentage of ash in the coal on average was 33 to 34%. Fly ash generation
in millions of tons therefore is 232.56 million tons and the utilization is about 92.4%.
These are the statistics from the Central Electricity Authority. From the Central
Electricity Authority, these are their statistics, which shows that fly ash utilization is
pretty high, it is almost 92%. And there are some issues that we have to be aware of
that it is okay to use fly ash bricks in the outdoor environment, but not in the indoor
environment.

We then come to the municipal solid waste law. The first box over here shows a court
case. This was a very famous court case between Almitra Patel, who is an
environmentalist versus the Union of India. She took the government to court and the
whole point of this case was to improve the solid waste management level. And I will
be talking more about that in the next lecture. This case was like I said a landmark
case and it led to the formulation of the municipal solid waste management and
handling rules in 2000. These rules were then amended, given a new name, solid
waste management rules in 2016. There are several ancillary rules that are
mentioned here as well as in previous slides. I will not go through all of them, but they
are all related in some manner to the solid waste issues.
So let us come to the municipal solid waste management and handling rules of 2000
and their revision in 2016. These rules apply to every municipal authority. The
municipal authority of a given area is held responsible for collection, segregation,
storage, transportation, processing, and disposal of municipal solid waste. What are
the main points? Main features are the vision for solid waste management for the
country is aligned in the revised rules with the Swachh Bharat Mission. One of the
biggest changes in the revised rule is segregation of waste. Segregation of waste at
the source is now mandatory as part of the revised rules. Waste generators have to
segregate waste into three fractions, biodegradable, non-biodegradable, and
domestic hazardous waste. And this has to be given to the waste collection team.
Door-to-door collection is also mandatory as part of the new solid waste management
rules. Sanitary waste has to be wrapped separately and included in non-
biodegradable waste. Biodegradable waste can be converted to compost and it has to
be co-marketed by the Department of Fertilizers with the fertilizers. The Ministry of
New and Renewable Energy will also facilitate creation of waste to energy
infrastructure for managing the biodegradable waste. So, supposing somebody wants
to set up a biogas plant, the Ministry of New and Renewable Energy will help with
that.
We then come to the batteries management and handling rules of which were first
enacted in 2001. They have been amended recently in 2022. These rules were initially
for lead acid batteries which are used in motor vehicles and inverters. Now the scope
of the batteries management and handling rules has been widened to include all types
of batteries regardless of their chemistry, shape, volume, weight, material
composition and use. There are some exceptions, batteries that are used for security
equipment or in space exploration missions. They are outside the purview of these
rules. All other batteries, whatever you use in your mobile phone versus whatever is
used in motor vehicles, industries and so many other applications, they are all under
the purview of these rules. Under the latest amendment, EPR has been brought into
effect. So EPR is extended producer responsibility. In this case, the producer is now
solely responsible for the collection, recycling and refurbishing of obligations and
targets that have been specified for different years in specific schedules attached to
the rules. These waste batteries have to be recycled or refurbished. They cannot be
landfilled and they cannot be incinerated. There is an online centralized portal that
has to be maintained by CPCB. All recyclers, refurbishers, producers, all involved in
the entire collection and disposal chain have to register on this online centralized
portal. They have to update this information and if there is any change, they have to
keep CPCB informed about it. The collection of waste batteries is the producer's
responsibility. Remember as part of the circular economy, the producer is now
responsible for the product to the end of its life and literally beyond because they
have to collect the waste batteries, they have to recycle or refurbish these batteries
and they can outsource this work to other agencies and so on, but it remains their
responsibility. The producer may operate any scheme that they deem fit including the
deposit refund scheme, buyback scheme or any other business model that helps them
to recover waste batteries.

Let us now come to the e-waste management and handling rules of 2011. They came
into effect in 2012, they were amended in 2015 and then again in 2022. These rules
apply to all people that are engaged in any aspect of electronic waste equipment in
terms of manufacturing, producing, refurbishing, dismantling, recycling, any aspect
of the entire chain of collection and disposal is under the purview of these rules. This
also includes not just the entire gadget or appliance, but its components,
consumables, parts and spares and it does not apply to the waste batteries, packaging
plastics, micro enterprise wherever they are involved and radioactive materials. For
the simple reason that these four categories have their own rules and therefore they
are exempt from the e-waste management and handling rules. In the e-waste rules of
2011, the extended producer responsibility was not clear. It has now been defined
very clearly in the amendment of 2022. There is a framework that is provided within
the rules, producer responsibility organizations have to be set up and the EPR is very
well defined. Depository fund scheme is a very popular scheme and this is part of the
e-waste rules. This is something that is common in other countries and now it has
been made part of the rules. A portion of the sale price will be retained by the
producers and will be refunded to the consumer when they return the product at the
end of its life and once the end of the life products are channelized according to the
prescribed methods. So, this makes it easier for the producer to collect their
electronic waste or electrical and electronic waste. Another new point in the amended
rules is that solar photovoltaic cells and panels are now under the purview of these
rules.

So, we now come to plastic waste management rules of 2016. These rules were last
amended on 6th July 2022. The salient features of the rule are carry-bags shall be
made in natural shade, they will be either colorless meaning transparent or they will
be white. They will not have any added pigments or if they are made using pigments
and colorants, they have to be in conformity with the Indian standard that is shown
over here. Another very important point is that carry bags have to be made of virgin
or recycled plastic and they should not be less than 120 microns in thickness. So, the
thickness of the carry bag is very important and they have to be extremely thick carry
bags. If the carry bags are made from compostable plastic, they have to conform to
the Indian standard code that is shown over here which is titled specifications for
compostable plastics and these standards can also be amended from time to time.
Recycled plastics or products made out of recycled plastics will not be used for
storing, carrying, dispensing or packaging food stuff. Sachets using plastic materials
shall not be used for storing, packing, selling gutkha, tobacco, pan masala and so on.
So, these materials are also not to be used. Another major feature of these amended
rules is the extended producer responsibility. If you remember the extended
producer responsibility was part of the electronic waste management rules only. This
has now been incorporated into the plastic waste management rules as well. It now
applies the same concept, the same rule applies to all producers, importers and brand
owners of plastic packaging.
Construction and demolition waste management rules came into effect on 29th March
2016. They apply to all waste from construction, repair and demolition activity
associated with any civil structure of whether it belongs to an individual organization,
authority, whatever the ownership may be and it may include any material, building
material, debris, rubble and so on. What are the criteria for selecting the sites for
processing of construction and demolition waste? Processing and recycling sites
should be large enough to last for 20 to 25 years. They should be far away from human
settlements, from forest areas, from water bodies, from monuments and places of
historic or religious interest. A buffer zone has to be maintained, the site has to be
fenced and separated from trespassers. Approach roads have to be made to avoid
dust. The site should be equipped with firefighting equipment and a way bridge and
a way bridge for monitoring of how much waste is coming in and how much is being
sold to recyclers and so on.
