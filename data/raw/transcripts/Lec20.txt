   Week 04(Lecture 05) : CLIMATE CHANGE (Adaption and Policy Responses)

Welcome to lecture 5 of week 4 of the NPTEL online certification course on Environmental
Science. In the last lecture, we explored the various mitigation options currently available
to tackle climate change. In this lecture, we will focus on how we can adapt to climate
change and familiarize ourselves with the various international treaties established to fight
climate change.
Specifically, in this lecture, we will cover the different adaptation strategies to combat
climate change, the various international strategies and agreements in place, and the
importance of the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) in enhancing our
understanding of climate change.

By the end of this lecture, you should be able to explain the concept of adapting to climate
change, understand the Kyoto Protocol and the Paris Agreement, grasp the concept of
carbon credits, and comprehend the role of the IPCC in climate science.
From our discussion so far, it is evident that climate change is upon us, and its impacts are
growing more severe with each passing year. Climate change is, in fact, one of the greatest
threats facing humanity, with far-reaching and devastating effects on people, the
environment, and the economy. Scientific evidence suggests that, despite advancements in
technology and other operational and economic measures to reduce greenhouse gas
emissions, the climate will continue to change, and the potential consequences may be
significant.

Given this, it is imperative that we invest in a massive effort to adapt to the conditions that
are now inevitable--higher temperatures, rising seas, fiercer storms, unpredictable rainfall,
and more acidic oceans. Adaptation, in this context, means adjusting our lives to
accommodate the changing climate and its associated challenges.

Adapting to climate change involves adjusting to the actual or expected future climate. The
primary goal of adaptation is to reduce our risks from the harmful effects of climate change,
such as sea level rise, more intense extreme weather events, or food insecurity.
Additionally, adaptation includes making the most of any potential beneficial opportunities
associated with climate change, such as longer growing seasons or increased crop yields in
certain regions.

When discussing climate change, it's essential to distinguish between adaptation and
mitigation. Adaptation refers to the process of adjusting to the changes that are already
happening or are expected to happen, focusing on minimizing the negative impacts and
taking advantage of any positive outcomes. On the other hand, mitigation involves efforts
to reduce or prevent the emission of greenhouse gases to slow down or halt the process of
climate change itself.
Adaptation, as we have seen, involves actions or changes in societal behavior aimed at
reducing or eliminating greenhouse gas emissions and/or removing these gases from the
atmosphere to prevent significant adverse climate effects. Adaptation, on the other hand,
refers to modifications in processes, practices, and infrastructures designed to prepare for
projected climate change effects and limit their adverse impacts to an acceptable level. In
essence, adaptation is a portfolio of actions intended to reduce, compensate for, or adapt to
the adverse impacts arising from changes in the Earth's climate.

Strategies for reducing risks from weather and climate extremes focus on either mitigating
the likelihood of extreme events by limiting greenhouse gas concentrations in the
atmosphere (which is mitigation) or by adapting the way built or natural infrastructure
responds to extreme events (which is adaptation). Often, a combination of both strategies
is employed. It's important to note that some greenhouse gases, especially carbon dioxide,
can persist in the atmosphere for centuries, making long-term planning and action essential.

So, even if humanity could immediately eliminate CO emissions, the excess amounts
already in the atmosphere--compared to pre-industrial levels--will lead to continued
warming and associated changes in climate for the foreseeable future. This inevitable
warming necessitates climate adaptation, and thus, both mitigation and adaptation must be
pursued in parallel. By mitigating the causes of climate change, we can reduce the costs
associated with weather and climate disasters and the expenses involved in adaptation.

The key to climate change adaptation lies in addressing the factors that make human and
non-human populations vulnerable, improving our ability to respond to climate challenges,
managing the risks associated with climate change, and directly confronting these issues
through collective and individual actions. Therefore, adaptation involves three core
components: reducing vulnerability to climate change, enhancing adaptive capacity, and
strengthening resilience.

Adaptation actions provide multiple benefits, with the foremost being avoided losses--the
ability of investments in adaptation to reduce future losses. This means that by taking
proactive measures to adapt to climate change, we can minimize the negative impacts and
prevent significant damages that would otherwise occur.

The second benefit of climate change adaptation is the positive economic impact, which
comes from reducing risks, increasing productivity, and driving innovation through the
need for adaptation. This economic benefit manifests in various ways, such as reducing
flood risks in urban areas, which lowers financial costs, enhances security, and makes
investments that would otherwise be too vulnerable to climate risks more viable. These
economic benefits not only protect assets but also foster growth and innovation by
encouraging the development of new technologies and practices tailored to cope with
climate challenges.
The third benefit revolves around social and environmental advantages. For instance,
mangrove forests offer significant protection against coastal flooding, providing over $80
billion per year in avoided losses and safeguarding nearly 18 million people. Beyond flood
protection, mangroves also contribute an estimated $40 to $50 billion annually in non-
market benefits related to fisheries, forestry, and recreation. By preserving and enhancing
these natural resources, we can derive substantial social and environmental benefits in
response to climate change.

When discussing climate change adaptation, it's essential to understand that it involves
three core components: reducing vulnerability, preparing for future climate impacts, and
restoring ecosystems and communities. These components lead to three key benefits:
avoided losses, economic benefits, and social and environmental benefits.

For example, implementing early warning systems can save lives and protect assets worth
at least ten times their cost. A simple 24-hour warning before a storm or heat wave can
reduce the resulting damage by at least 30 percent. Such systems are a critical part of
reducing vulnerability and preparing for extreme weather events.

Moreover, adaptation strategies are not limited to local applications; they have broader
implications at sub-national, national, and even regional scales. Effective adaptation
requires coordinated efforts across these levels to ensure that the benefits of adaptation--
whether economic, social, or environmental--are maximized and distributed equitably.
This comprehensive approach ensures that communities and ecosystems are resilient to the
impacts of climate change, securing both current and future generations.

For instance, an agriculture ministry setting farm policies, a water ministry investing in
watershed development, or a public works department strengthening national grids all
operate beyond the scope of local implementation, impacting national or even regional
adaptation strategies. A compelling example of this broader approach is Bangladesh, which
has effectively reduced the number of deaths from climate disasters by implementing
comprehensive measures. These include scaling up early warning systems, improving
disaster response with cyclone shelters, raising civic awareness, strengthening building
infrastructure, and enhancing post-disaster recovery processes.

By following a similar approach, other regions can also adapt to the long-term negative
impacts of climate change. In fact, much can be learned from nature in this regard.

Nature provides invaluable and irreplaceable protection against the mounting impacts of
climate change. For example, forests play a critical role in storing and regulating water, a
service that will become increasingly vital as rainfall patterns become more erratic.
Wetlands act as natural sponges, soaking up potentially deadly floods and ensuring a steady
supply of water to farmers and cities during times of drought. Mangroves, too, serve as
natural barriers, taming powerful storm surges that could otherwise devastate coastal
communities. These crucial ecosystems support entire economies and societies by
providing food, fuel, and livelihoods while also combating climate change by capturing
carbon from the atmosphere.

The protection and restoration of these natural systems are not just local concerns; they
have far-reaching implications that extend to national and regional levels. Investing in the
health and resilience of these ecosystems is a vital part of adapting to climate change,
ensuring that they continue to provide the essential services that communities rely on,
especially as the climate becomes more unpredictable. By understanding and leveraging
the natural defenses provided by forests, wetlands, mangroves, and other ecosystems, we
can enhance our collective resilience to climate change's inevitable challenges.

A thriving natural environment is indeed a cornerstone of building resilience across all
sectors. We can already see the immense opportunities that nature offers in enhancing
societal resilience in various landscapes, from uplands to the ocean. For example, restoring
upland forests and watersheds could save water utilities in the world's 534 largest cities an
estimated 890 million dollars annually, while also playing a crucial role in regulating water
flows and managing the more extreme floods expected in the future. Similarly, lakes,
marshes, and river floodplains can help slow the release of floodwaters and filter out
sediments, thereby reducing the impact of floods.

Governments and companies need to recognize the value of these natural assets and
identify their potential to support adaptation and resilience strategies. Climate scientists,
since the mid-20th century, have gathered detailed observations of various weather
phenomena--such as temperature changes, precipitation patterns, and storm activities--
and their influence on climate factors like ocean currents and atmospheric chemical
composition. These data clearly indicate that Earth's climate has changed over almost every
conceivable time scale since the beginning of geologic time, and that human activities,
particularly since the Industrial Revolution, have increasingly influenced the pace and
extent of present-day climate change.

In response to the growing conviction within the scientific community, the
Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) was established in 1988 by the World
Meteorological Organization and the United Nations Environment Programme. The IPCC
was created to provide policymakers and decision-makers with regular scientific
assessments on climate change, its implications, and potential future risks. It also offers
recommendations for adaptation and mitigation strategies to help manage and combat the
impacts of climate change effectively.
Simultaneously, various other international bodies, including the United Nations General
Assembly, recognized the necessity for international cooperation on climate change to
adopt effective measures within a global framework. The United Nations General
Assembly, during its 45th session in 1990, acknowledged the findings of the IPCC and
decided to initiate negotiations for an effective framework convention on climate change.
The aim was to complete these negotiations before the UN Conference on Environment
and Development in June 1992, known as the Rio Summit, which we discussed in week 2
of this course.

The outcome of these negotiations was the United Nations Framework Convention on
Climate Change (UNFCCC), which was signed in 1992 at the Rio Summit. The UNFCCC
serves as the foundational climate agreement, providing a platform for most subsequent
international climate agreements. This convention has been ratified by a wide cross-section
of both developed and developing countries, including the United States. The primary goal
of the UNFCCC is to prevent dangerous human interference with the climate system by
stabilizing greenhouse gas concentrations in the atmosphere at a level that would prevent
hazardous anthropogenic impacts on the climate.
To enhance the effectiveness of the UNFCCC, the Kyoto Protocol was adopted in
December 1997, named after the Japanese city where it was agreed upon. The Kyoto
Protocol aimed to reduce the emissions of greenhouse gases that contribute to global
warming. It officially came into force in 2005, and its key objective was to cut the
emissions of six greenhouse gases--carbon dioxide, methane, nitrous oxide,
hydrofluorocarbons, perfluorocarbons, and sulfur hexafluoride--to 5.2 percent below the
1990 levels during the commitment period of 2008 to 2012.

Under the Kyoto Protocol, it was mandatory for most Annex I signatories, which included
mainly industrialized nations and several countries with economies in transition, to reduce
their emissions. However, developing countries were not required to limit their emissions.
While the Kyoto Protocol was a landmark diplomatic achievement, its success was
uncertain. Reports from the first two years after the treaty took effect indicated that most
participants were likely to fail in meeting their emission targets. Moreover, critics argued
that even if the targets were met, the ultimate environmental benefits would be minimal.

One significant point of contention was that China, the world's leading emitter of
greenhouse gases, and the United States, the second-largest emitter, were not bound by the
protocol. China was classified as a developing country at that time, and the United States
had not ratified the protocol. This led to further criticism, suggesting that the emission
reductions proposed by the protocol were too modest to have a significant impact on global
temperatures in the following decades, even if fully implemented with U.S. participation.
Additionally, some developing countries argued that adapting to climate variability and
change was just as crucial as reducing greenhouse gas emissions. Taking these
considerations into account, delegates at the 18th Conference of the Parties (COP 18) held
in Doha, Qatar, in 2012, agreed to extend the Kyoto Protocol until 2020.

During the extended period under the Kyoto Protocol, countries committed to collectively
reduce emissions by at least 18 percent below 1990 levels. This extension also reaffirmed
the pledge made at COP 17, held in Durban, South Africa, in 2011, to create a new,
comprehensive, legally binding climate treaty by 2015. This treaty would require all
greenhouse gas-producing countries, including major emitters not bound by the Kyoto
Protocol, such as China, India, and the United States, to limit and reduce their emissions
of carbon dioxide and other greenhouse gases.

However, the years that followed were marked by a series of conferences that often ended
in disagreements. Eventually, at COP 21, held in Paris in 2015, delegates signed a global
but non-binding agreement known as the Paris Agreement. This landmark accord aimed to
limit the increase in the world's average temperature to no more than 2 degrees Celsius
above pre-industrial levels, while striving to keep this increase to 1.5 degrees Celsius.
The Paris Agreement, signed by all 196 signatories of the UNFCCC, effectively replaced
the Kyoto Protocol. It mandated a progress review every five years and called for the
creation of a fund exceeding 100 billion dollars by 2020, which would be replenished
annually to help developing countries adopt non-greenhouse gas-producing technologies.
The Paris Agreement was a significant achievement for the international community
because, for the first time, it brought all countries together in an ambitious effort to combat
climate change by limiting global temperature rise to well below 2 degrees Celsius and
striving for 1.5 degrees Celsius. Each year, parties to the convention meet in Conferences
of the Parties (COP) and technical meetings throughout the year to advance the aims and
ambitions of the Paris Agreement and achieve progress in its implementation.

Both the Kyoto Protocol and the Paris Agreement were concluded under the UNFCCC
with the objective of stabilizing greenhouse gas concentrations in the atmosphere and
preventing dangerous human interference with the climate system. However, the Paris
Agreement became a global treaty in the true sense of the term due to several key
differences:

1. Scope of Participation: Unlike the Kyoto Protocol, which required only developed
countries to reduce emissions, the Paris Agreement recognized climate change as a shared
global problem and called on all countries, both developed and developing, to set emission
targets.

2. Inclusivity: The Kyoto Protocol did not compel developing countries, including major
carbon emitters like China and India, to take action. In contrast, the Paris Agreement called
for all countries to contribute to the global effort, regardless of their development status.

These differences made the Paris Agreement more inclusive and reflective of the global
nature of the climate challenge, establishing it as a pivotal treaty in the fight against climate
change.
The United States signed the Kyoto Protocol in 1998 but never ratified it and eventually
withdrew its signature. In contrast, the Paris Agreement, which now has 196 parties,
requires all countries, whether developed or developing, to reduce their emissions.
Governments set targets known as nationally determined contributions (NDCs) with the
goal of preventing the global average temperature from rising more than 2 degrees Celsius
above pre-industrial levels, while also pursuing efforts to keep the temperature rise below
1.5 degrees Celsius.
Both the Kyoto Protocol and the Paris Agreement provided several mechanisms for
countries to reach their emission reduction targets. One of the most efficient ways for
governments to reduce greenhouse gas emissions and drive innovation in decarbonization
technologies is through carbon pricing.

Carbon pricing, or the carbon market, requires emitters to obtain an emission allowance
for each ton of greenhouse gases they release into the atmosphere and surrender them to
the government at the end of each compliance period, typically three years. These
allowances, known as carbon credits, function like permission slips for emissions. When a
company buys a carbon credit, usually from the government, it gains permission to emit
one ton of CO.
The carbon credit system also allows for revenue to flow from companies to regulators.
Companies that have excess credits (due to emitting less than their allowed limit) can sell
these credits to other companies that exceed their emission limits.
For example, imagine two companies, Company 1 and Company 2, both of which are
allowed to emit 300 tons of carbon annually. However, Company 1 is on track to emit 400
tons of carbon, while Company 2 will only emit 200 tons. To avoid penalties such as fines
and extra taxes, Company 1 can purchase 100 tons of carbon credits from Company 2,
which has 100 tons of emission allowance to spare.
The government establishes annual greenhouse gas emission caps, which are the maximum
emission limits. These caps are progressively lowered over time to encourage greenhouse
gas emission reductions. The government sells emission units at auction four times a year,
and only registered emitters and participants in the carbon market can participate in these
auctions. Emitters that can reduce their greenhouse gas emissions below their allocated
units, perhaps by improving production efficiency or adopting cleaner, green technologies,
can sell their surplus emission units on the carbon market to other emitters who exceed
their allocations.
This market-based approach incentivizes companies to reduce their emissions and
encourages innovation in cleaner technologies, helping to meet the broader goals of
international climate agreements like the Paris Agreement.

The government also allows emitters to compensate for a part of their greenhouse gas
emissions by using offset credits. As already mentioned, the findings of the
Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) played a decisive role in leading to
the UNFCCC and continue to be a major source of information for the negotiation and
conceptualization of international treaties addressing climate change.
When the IPCC was first established by the World Meteorological Organization and the
United Nations Environment Program (UNEP) in 1988, its key objective was to provide
the world with a clear scientific view of the current state of knowledge on climate change
and its potential environmental and socio-economic impacts. In this respect, the IPCC has
been highly successful, and today it stands as the leading international body for the
assessment of climate change. Any country that is a member of UNEP is eligible to join
the IPCC, and currently, 195 countries are members.

Importantly, the IPCC does not conduct any research of its own or monitor the climate.
Instead, it reviews and assesses the most recent scientific, technical, and socio-economic
information related to climate change. The organization is divided into three working
groups and a task force on greenhouse gas inventories.
Working Group 1 deals with the physical science basis of climate change, analyzing
observational records, forcing agents that cause climate change, and the response of the
climate system to these agents. It also addresses the question of human influence on today's
climate and projections for future climate scenarios.
Working Group 2 assesses vulnerabilities and adaptation possibilities for major sectors and
regions of the world. It explores the implications of climate change for sustainable
development.
Working Group 3 examines mitigation options in various sectors, their costs, co-benefits,
barriers, opportunities, policies, measures, and instruments. It places climate change
mitigation within the context of sustainable development.
The Task Force on Greenhouse Gas Inventories develops and refines methods for
calculating and reporting on national greenhouse gas emissions and removals.
The IPCC's mandate is to assess the state of scientific literature on all aspects of climate
change, its impacts, and society's options for responding to it. Whenever a new piece of
scientific research relevant to these topics is published, it becomes part of the body of
evidence that the IPCC assesses. The IPCC does not conduct original research itself, nor
does it develop its own models or scenarios. Instead, it provides governments at all levels
with scientific information they can use to develop climate policies.

IPCC assessments are a key input into international negotiations to tackle climate change.
The reports produced by the IPCC are drafted and reviewed in several stages to ensure
objectivity and transparency. The IPCC assesses thousands of scientific papers published
each year, summarizing what we know and what we do not know about the risks related to
climate change, helping policymakers make informed decisions.

The IPCC identifies areas of agreement within the scientific community, highlights
differences of opinion, and indicates where further research is necessary. It does not
conduct its own research but instead mobilizes hundreds of scientists from diverse
backgrounds worldwide to produce its reports. The IPCC's main outputs are regular
assessment reports that summarize the current state of knowledge on climate change. The
most recent of these, the Sixth Assessment Report, was published between 2021 and 2023.
This report includes four volumes covering the physical science basis, impacts, adaptation
and vulnerability, mitigation of climate change, and a synthesis report.
The synthesis report provides an integrated summary of the most critical information
contained in the three other volumes. The IPCC also produces special reports, which are
more focused than the main assessments, addressing cross-disciplinary issues that span
more than one working group. To date, the IPCC has published 14 special reports on topics
such as carbon capture and storage, renewable energy, and the influence of climate change
on oceans and the cryosphere.

The IPCC also provides guidelines for national greenhouse gas inventories, offering
methodologies for estimating national inventories of anthropogenic emissions and
removals by sinks of greenhouse gases. The impact of each report has significantly
influenced our understanding and perception of climate change. For example, the evidence
published in the First Assessment Report in 1990 highlighted the importance of
international cooperation in tackling climate change, which led to the formation of the
UNFCCC, an international treaty aimed at reducing global warming and managing its
consequences.
The Second Assessment Report, published in 1995, concluded that the balance of evidence
suggested a discernible human influence on the Earth's climate. This conclusion strongly
influenced negotiators and played a pivotal role in the decision to adopt the Kyoto Protocol
in 1997, which extended the UNFCCC by pledging developed nations to reduce emissions
by an average of 5% during the 2008 to 2012 period.
In a similar manner, the Fifth Assessment Report provided the key scientific foundation
for the Paris Agreement. Notably, in 2007, the IPCC, along with former U.S. Vice
President Al Gore, was awarded the Nobel Peace Prize for their efforts in raising awareness
about human-induced climate change and laying the groundwork for necessary
countermeasures. The IPCC continues to uphold its mission, providing critical insights into
climate change.

Throughout this week, we've explored various facets of climate change, from the evidence
of its existence, the key drivers, the long-term consequences, mitigation options, adaptation
strategies, and the policies we have revisited. To summarize our week-long discussion on
climate change, we can confidently state that human activities, primarily through the
emission of greenhouse gases, have unequivocally caused global warming. Global
greenhouse gas emissions have continued to rise due to unsustainable energy use, land use,
lifestyle choices, and patterns of consumption and production across different regions,
countries, and individuals. This has led to widespread and rapid changes in the atmosphere,
oceans, cryosphere, and biosphere. Human-caused climate change is already influencing
weather and climate extremes in every region globally.

Rapid and far-reaching transitions across all sectors and systems are necessary to achieve
deep and sustained emissions reductions and to secure a livable and sustainable future for
all. Numerous options are available to reduce emission-intensive consumption, including
behavioral and lifestyle changes, which also have co-benefits for societal well-being. The
choices we make today will have repercussions for hundreds, even thousands of years.
Therefore, it is imperative that we make our choices wisely and start taking action at the
local level. On that note, I conclude our discussion on climate change.
