So, let us start with the basic principles and doctrines that are the basis of Supreme Court
judgments related to the environment. So, the doctrine of absolute liability is the first one.
The owners of the industry or the authorities are fully liable for granting compensation to
the victims of any environmental tragedy or disaster due to them. And they have to pay for
restoration of the environment to the original state as far as possible. We then come to the
second major principle and that is the Polluter pays principle. That means that the polluter
whether it is an industry, whether it is a factory, whether it is the municipal authority, they
are fully liable for the cost of remediating pollution resulting from past or present activities.
The third principle is the precautionary principle. In this case, the judges can examine a
case on the basis of potential threat. Potential threat means based on our scientific
understanding at present. If the scientific community and the legal community feel that
there is a sound basis for thinking that there is a threat to the environment or to human
health, then based on that and on the burden of proof. So, it is also called weight of evidence
or burden of proof lies with the proposer. So, the proposer has to prove that there will be
no threat to the environment or to human health. And if any threat is perceived, then on the
basis of precautionary principle, the judges can either reject or accept the case depending
on the burden of proof. The next principle is the doctrine of sustainable development. We
have been looking at the idea, the concept of sustainable development throughout this
course. This is also one of the major principles that guides Supreme Court of India's
judgments related to the environment. So, sustainable development for improving the
quality of life, eliminating poverty and ensuring that natural assets are not exhausted in a
single generation, these are the basis of the doctrine of sustainable development. The last
one is the public trust doctrine. The public trust doctrine states that the state, state meaning
the state or the centre is the trustee of the commons and the commons include air, water,
sea and forests. These cannot be subject to private ownership and they have to be protected
for public use. So, these are the five basic principles that guide Supreme Court of India's
judgments vis-a-vis the environment.
Let us start with the oldest one. This was a very famous court case by a Sri Vardhichand
and a group of people against the municipality of Ratlam. This judgment came in 1980.
The residents of Ratlam municipality were frustrated about the lack of sanitary facilities
and the street litter that was lying around in the city. They took the municipality to court
under Section 133 of the Criminal Procedure Code for Public Nuisance. So, Ratlam
municipalities obligations under Section 123 of the Madhya Pradesh Municipalities Act of
1961 include providing sanitary facilities and preventing street contamination coming from
a nearby alcohol manufacturing plant. The municipality argued in its defense that the
residents have chosen to live where they are living and knowing fully well that these
facilities are not available. The second defense was, the second argument was that the
authorities did not have funds to construct the sanitary facilities required to comply with
these laws. The Supreme Court of India decided that the municipality cannot claim inability
to provide basic sanitation facilities. They have to prevent street contamination and lack of
financial ability is not a good excuse. So, the municipality was ordered to take immediate
action with regards to contamination and pollution in the streets. They were ordered to
provide sanitary facilities and services for their residents within a six month period.
We then come to the second landmark judgment. All of you would be aware of the Bhopal
gas tragedy which happened on 2nd and 3rd December in 1984. Almost a year later, there
was a leak of oleum gas in Kirti Nagar, Delhi on 4th and 6th December 1985. The leaks
came from Shriram foods and fertilizers. M.C. Mehta who is an advocate and an
environmentalist along with others took the Union of India to court in 1986 and this case
became famous because of the concept of public liability. The concept of public or strict
liability was already there, but after the oleum leakage case, this judgment of the Supreme
Court of India became a landmark judgment because the principle of strict liability was
modified to the doctrine of absolute liability. So, in a sense it made legal history at this
point. The court held that the permission for carrying out any hazardous industry close to
human habitation cannot be given and the industry has to be relocated. So, that was a major
landmark. The Environment Protection Act 1986 came into effect after these two tragedies,
Bhopal as well as Delhi, these were the two gas tragedies and after that the Environment
Protection Act which had overriding powers, which gave overriding powers to CPCB as
well as to the Ministry of Environment and Forest. This became a major regulation and this
came in after all these cases. The Environment Protection Act is the basic concept
underlying the Environment Protection Act is the need for development alongside the need
for protecting human life and the environment. So, balancing these two requirements of
industrial development as well as protecting human life and environment became part of
the legal set of rules and regulations.
Then came another very famous court case and this was in 1987. This was known as the
Dhoon Valley case. An NGO, Rural Litigation and Entitlement Kendra (RLEK) based in
Dehradun took the state of Uttar Pradesh to court and like I said in common language, this
case became known as the Dhoon Valley case. Now, the Dhoon Valley is very famous for
its pristine environment, for its beauty and many others, its wildlife and so on. What was
also happening in a major way was limestone quarrying. So, this is a limestone quarry
photo that you can see over here and in these limestone quarries, dynamite was being used
and that dynamite would result in landslides. There were several deaths of local people
because of the landslides caused by the dynamite use in these areas. This case became a
landmark case mainly because of the doctrine of sustainable development that was upheld
by the Supreme Court of India for the first time in our country. So, the court held in 1987
that all mines in this area except three will have to be closed. Mining in the valley was held
to have violated the Forest Conservation Act of 1980. The Supreme Court made a statement
that the economic development in that area should not come at the cost of environmental
degradation. They also held that permanent assets of mankind are not to be exhausted in
one generation. The natural resources should be used with requisite attention and care so
that ecology and environment cannot be affected in any serious way. The constitution of
India also guarantees the right to wholesome environment as a fundamental human right
under article 21. So, this particular right was also part of the Supreme Court's judgment.
Then we come to another landmark judgment in 1988. Here again, environmentalist and
advocate MC Mehta took the Union of India to court about the pollution of the Ganga River
by tanneries. Now tanneries as many of you may be knowing are affluent generating
industries and the affluent from these tanneries has very high COD, BOD, heavy metals
and several other pollutants. So, because these tanneries were emitting this wastewater
discharge without treatment, there was zero treatment at that time. So, this writ petition
was filed in the Supreme Court. The justice at that point gave a historic judgment ordering
closure of a large number of polluting tanneries in the Kanpur area. It was observed in this
judgment that just like any industry cannot be allowed to exist if it does not pay minimum
wages to its workers. Similarly, a tannery cannot be allowed to exist if it does not have a
primary treatment plant. And today we have much stricter norms. There are discharge
standards, there are affluent discharge standards and all industries including tanneries have
to abide by these discharge standards.
We then come to some more landmark judgments that came because of MC Mehta's
advocacy as well as environmentalism. So, I have summarized a couple of his cases over
here. And this was in 1991. He took again the Union of India to court and the objective
was to create environmental awareness and include it in the education of all citizens. So,
to make that point cinema theatres all over the country have to exhibit two slides free of
cost related to the environment in each show. Their licenses to operate can be cancelled if
they fail to do so. The television network in the country has to give 5 to 7 minutes to televise
programs related to the environment as well as having a regular weekly program on the
environment. The environment has to be a compulsory subject up to 12 standards from the
academic session of 1992 onwards. The university grants commission UGC will also have
to introduce the subject in higher classes in different universities. So, you can see that
environmental science and studies became an integral part of the curriculum of all
disciplines and at all levels because of this particular landmark judgment. MC Mehta also
won other landmark judgments from the SCI including banning of lead from gasoline.
Because of these activist legal cases and the landmark judgments that came about because
of that he has received the Goldman environmental prize, the Raman Magsaysay award
and the Padma Shri at various times in the past.
We now come to another very famous case and that is the Taj Trapezium case. Again,
advocate MC Mehta took the Union of India to court in 1987 and a historic landmark
judgment was delivered by the Supreme Court in 1996. As you all know the Taj Mahal is
the pride of India, it is one of the wonders of the world and it was facing a threat to its not
just its beauty, but also to its structural stability. This was mainly due to high toxic
emissions from Mathura refineries. There were several iron foundries, glass and other
chemical industries in this region. So, in the photo on the right, you can see a map showing
you the Taj Trapezium. This area has not just the Taj Mahal, it has several other very
famous monuments. It is got Fatehpur Sikri, it is got Agra Fort and 255 other historic
monuments exist within this particular area. Acid rain caused by emissions, air emissions
from these refineries, iron foundries and so many other industries which were operating in
these areas were a serious threat to these historic monuments. The petitioners stated that
the marble was yellowing and the inner chamber of the Taj had fungal growth. So, these
were some of the visible indicators of pollution that were destroying the beauty of the Taj
Mahal. The Supreme Court in its orders gave directions to basically banning the use of coal
in this particular area. They directed the industries to switch to compressed natural gas and
the tanneries were asked to relocate out of the Taj Trapezium zone. A green belt was
created around the Mathura refinery for protecting the environment. There are many details
in each one of these cases that I am talking about that are available in the full text of the
judgments. What you see in these slides are very brief summaries of each one of these
landmark judgments.
Let us come to another case. Now in Vellore, a citizen's welfare forum was formed and
they took the Union of India to court and the SCI gave its judgment in 1996. This one was
again related to tanneries. So, river Palar in the state of Tamil Nadu was getting polluted
because of tanneries and other industries operating along the river. Palar river is the main
source of drinking and bathing water for villages and towns in this region. The Tamil Nadu
Agricultural University Research Centre in Vellore found that approximately 35,000
hectares of agricultural land has become either completely or partially unsuitable for
cultivation. In its judgment, the Supreme Court applied the principles, `Polluter Pays
Principle' and the `Doctrine of Sustainable Development' and they analysed the
relationship between environment and industry. There were serious arguments on both
sides. The tanneries were major foreign exchange earners as well as major employers for a
large number of workers in this particular region and because of that, things had been
progressing the way they were. Because of contamination of the water as well as the soil,
it was becoming evident that this was a threat to human health as well as the environment.
So, the Supreme Court ruled against the tanneries. Each tannery had to pay 10,000 as fine
in the collector's office and they all had to contribute to the setting up of a common effluent
treatment plant.
The next landmark judgment that I have here is again by Advocate M.C. Mehta against
Kamal Nath and others. So, in this particular case, the Supreme Court held that the
Himachal Pradesh government had violated the doctrine of public trust. They gave their
judgment in 1996. The Himachal Pradesh state government had leased a protected forest
area on the bank of river Beas to a motel company for commercial purposes. This motel
company was owned by either Kamal Nath or his family members and the motivation for
running the company was commercial only. In 1996, the Supreme Court passed a judgment
that held the state responsible for maintaining natural resources. There is a quotation here
at the bottom, our legal system. This is a quotation from the Supreme Court's judgment-
Our legal system which is based on English common law includes the public trust doctrine
as part of its jurisprudence. The state is the trustee of all natural resources which by nature
are meant for public use and enjoyment. Public at large is the beneficiary of the seashore
running waters, air, forest and ecologically fragile lands. The state as a trustee is under a
legal duty to protect natural resources. These resources are meant for public use and cannot
be converted into private ownership.
The Andhra Pradesh Pollution Control Board was taken to court by another group and the
group was led by Professor M. V. Nayudu. Judgment I think was passed in 1999. The
proposed project was a new factory for the production of vegetable oils and this is the
group. Professor Nayudu and others was the group that was proposing this project, this
industry, setting up of an industry for production of vegetable oil. They had purchased 12
acres of land in Peddashapur which was Andhra Pradesh at that time, it is present day
Telangana. Vegetable oils are category red hazardous materials and the proposed area of
this project was within 10 km radius of two major drinking water reservoirs. So, we have
the Himayat Sagar and Osman Sagar which provided water to the twin cities of Hyderabad
and Secunderabad. So, the location of the industry and the category of the industry put it
in the red category. The Andhra Pradesh Pollution Control Board did not give consent to
operate this industry on these two grounds and the respondent industry took them to court
to question the validity of their orders. The Supreme Court of India relied on three major
principles, the precautionary principle, the polluter pays principle and the burden of proof.
The precautionary principle is based on the need for authorities to prevent environmental
pollution from happening based on sound scientific principles as well as knowledge. The
court in its judgment directed that the authority which means APPCB has to be appointed
under the Environment Protection Act and it shall implement this precautionary principle.
Further, when a new proposal is envisaged and a risk of great or irreversible damage to the
environment is highly probable, it is then the burden of responsibility for providing proof
is on the proposer of the project. So, the burden of proof lies on the one that is proposing
to undertake the activity in question. SCI at this point also acknowledged the need for
getting technical and scientific expertise along with legal expertise. This judgment
eventually led to the formation of the National Green Tribunal which was going to deal
with environment related matters only and they would base their judgments on these
principles as well as natural justice.

So, we come to the last one of the landmark judgments. This was another case in which in
common language it is called the Godavarman2 case in which a group of people took the
Union of India to court and the Supreme Court passed a series of directions after 1995 until
the final judgment in 2014. What was at stake was the livelihood of forest dwellers in the
Nilgiri region of Tamil Nadu and the fact that the forests were being destroyed. The
Supreme Court decided to set up a compensatory afforestation fund management and
planning authority or CAMPA for short to monitor the afforestation efforts to oversee the
compensation of those who suffered on account of deforestation and to accelerate activities
for the preservation of natural forest. A similar writ petition was filed by the Tarun Bharat
Sangh in the Supreme Court to stop mining activities in the Sareska Wildlife Sanctuary in
Rajasthan. This was in 1991. So, after this writ petition was filed the Supreme Court of
India banned all mining activities in all sanctuaries in the country. Subsequently all mining
and developmental activities within a 1 km buffer zone around national parks and
sanctuaries were banned by the Supreme Court of India.
