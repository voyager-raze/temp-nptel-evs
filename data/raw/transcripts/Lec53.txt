The first thing is genetic. Within a single species, there can be thousands of strains. And
when you have cultivated or cultured species, we call them cultivars. So for example, in
India, we have different varieties of mangoes, thousands of different varieties exist.

We have 50,000 types of rice. So these are cultivars or cultured strains within a particular
species. So that is genetic biodiversity. Within a species, you may have groups of
organisms that have individuals that are capable of exchanging genes and interbreeding
and so on. So for example, there are only three known species of peacocks or peafowl
that are known in the world.

One is in India, the other is in Indonesia, and the third is in Congo in Africa. So these are
the same species that inhabit different parts of the world. And because they are isolated
from each other, they have become very different. They look very different. And then we
have ecological biodiversity.

This ecological biodiversity is reflected in very large areas. So, where you have large
countries like India, the US, Canada, China, so on, they occupy large areas and there is a
diversity within the ecosystem. So, you have different types of ecosystems within the
same country. But then you also have very small Scandinavian countries where the
diversity in terms of types of ecosystems is very limited. So, for example, in India, we
have deserts, we have alpine types of vegetation and flora and fauna in the Himalayas.

The first one is bioaccumulation. Let us imagine that you have an ecosystem where there
is a toxic compound or contaminant in the environment. This contaminant is going to
over a period of time, the organisms that are exposed to that contaminant will find that it
is building up in the tissue of various organisms within that ecosystem, the contaminant
level is building up over a period of time. And because it is building up over a period of
time, we call it bioaccumulation. So, increase in concentration of a chemical in the tissue
of an organism over time, it can happen by three different exposure pathways.
It can be through inhalation, it can be through the skin, dermal pathway or through
dietary ingestion. It all depends on the nature of the organism, how it obtains its food and
what are the roots of exposure. These are the roots of exposure. Then we come to
biomagnification. When you have an increase in the contaminant level in the tissue as
you go up the trophic pyramid.

So I will show you examples in the next few slides of exactly that. So here you have not
the primary producers, but you have the first consumer over here. And from the first
consumer to the second consumer, third and fourth consumer, you find that the
contaminant level in the tissue of that series of organisms is increasing. And why does
that happen? We will take a look at that in the next few slides. The third definition is
bioconcentration.

Let us say you have a particular contaminant in water. And there are several organisms
within that ecosystem that are exposed to that water. The nature of that contaminant will
result in accumulation, which we have already seen over a period of time. But the
increase in concentration compared to the concentration in water is what we call
bioconcentration factor. So, C is the concentration in the tissue of that organism.

It can be fish, it can be any higher organism or even lower organism. It can be some kind

of insect, whatever it is. That concentration in the tissue of that particular organism

divided by the concentration in water is what we define as the bioconcentration factor.

So, this is called eutrophication. So, eutrophication is defined as the excessive growth of

eutrophication because it is an accelerated natural eutrophication process because human
activities are causing more nutrients to go into these natural water bodies. So there is an
acceleration of the water quality decline.
All these lakes accumulate silt and organic matter. It leads to increase in nutrient levels. It

content which means low primary productivity.
