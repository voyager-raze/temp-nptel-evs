There is widespread scientific consensus and overwhelming political agreement that
developing and deploying a broad portfolio of advanced energy technologies would be the
most suitable, potentially effective, and sustainable approach to achieve long-term
stabilization of CO levels in the atmosphere. Energy efficiency improvements and
increased use of clean renewable energy resources, such as solar, wind, ocean, geothermal,
hydroelectricity, and other non-fossil fuel-based resources, such as nuclear energy, are key
components of this portfolio. In the short to medium term, carbon capture and storage
(CCS) is a viable technological option for mitigating anthropogenic energy-related CO
emissions.
Carbon capture and storage, which has recently evolved into carbon capture, utilization,
and storage (CCUS), is an integrated technology that aims to selectively remove CO from
fossil fuel gas streams of fossil-fired power plants and other large industrial sources. It then
safely deposits the captured CO in secure storage sites or reuses it as a raw material in
industrial processes, thereby keeping it out of the atmosphere for a prolonged period.
CCUS comprises four distinct components:

    1. Capturing CO from the smokestacks of fossil-fired power plants and other large
         industrial sources, such as cement industries, fertilizer industries, steel industries,
         etc.

    2. Compressing the captured CO to a liquid-like state called supercritical fluid and
         transporting it to suitable sites, which could be onshore or offshore.

    3. Directly utilizing the compressed CO as a feedstock in several important industrial
         sectors, such as manufacturing fertilizers like urea, the food and beverage industry,
         and the manufacture of value-added products.

    4. Storing the CO using natural or engineered trapping mechanisms and monitoring
         as necessary.

Globally, there are more than 8000 CO point sources that can conceivably adopt carbon
capture utilization and storage technology as a means of delivering deep and sustained
emissions reduction. However, the technical difficulties of extracting CO with high purity
and recovery from flue gas streams, as well as the expensive operations involved with the
separation process, can severely impede the large-scale implementation of CO.

In general, there are three basic approaches to capturing CO from the combustion of fossil
fuels. These are pre-combustion CO capture, which involves CO removal prior to
combustion of the fossil fuel; oxy-fuel combustion systems, which involve combustion of
the fossil fuel in pure oxygen to get a highly concentrated CO waste gas; and post-
combustion CO capture, which involves CO removal from the flue gas stream following
normal combustion.
Let's look into each of these combustion technologies in a little more detail. Pre-
combustion capture involves removing the carbon content of the fuel prior to combustion.
In this process, a primary fuel, which could be coal, oil, natural gas, or even biomass, is
first converted into syngas, which is a mixture of carbon dioxide, carbon monoxide, and
hydrogen. This syngas is then reacted with steam by the process of steam reforming or with
oxygen, known as partial oxidation or gasification, to convert the syngas into a mixture of
carbon dioxide and hydrogen. This mixture of carbon dioxide and hydrogen is called the
water-gas shift reaction. The CO can be separated from this mixture, while the hydrogen
can then be burnt to produce power. The main advantage of pre-combustion capture is that
the flue gas produced contains a high concentration of CO at very high pressures (20 to
80 bars), reducing CO compression costs. Additionally, the process can simultaneously
remove other acidic contaminants such as sulfur oxides, nitrogen oxides, and particulate
matter, which are criteria air pollutants.
However, the key problem with pre-combustion capture is that it requires high capital
investment and cannot be retrofitted to existing coal-fired power plants. Pulverized coal-
fired power plants make up much of the world's installed base of fossil fuel power. Oxy-
fuel combustion is a near zero-emission technology because it involves burning fossil fuels
in almost pure oxygen instead of air, using a cryogenic air separation unit or membranes.
Since nitrogen is removed prior to combustion, the flue gas that leaves the combustion unit
contains only carbon dioxide and water with trace amounts of SOx and NOx. The flue gas
mixture is then cooled and cleaned to remove SOx and NOx, and a further compression
step separates the carbon dioxide and water to produce a high-purity CO stream ready for
sequestration. The primary benefit of oxy-fuel combustion technology is the significant
reduction in both the size and capital cost of a fossil-fired power plant compared to
conventional air-based combustion systems. Another potential benefit is the low NOx
emission due to the absence of nitrogen in the flame, which also removes the requirement
for a NOx separation unit. However, the main drawback is the need for large volumes of
high-purity oxygen, which imposes a high capital cost and energy penalty.
Post-combustion carbon capture involves capturing CO from flue gases produced by
burning fossil fuels in ambient air. When fossil fuels such as coal, oil, or natural gas are
combusted in ambient air, the process results in a gas containing about 70 to 80 percent
nitrogen, 10 to 15 percent carbon dioxide, 3 to 4 percent oxygen, and trace amounts of
SOx, NOx, particulate matter, carbon monoxide, and mercury. Instead of being directly
discharged to the atmosphere, this flue gas undergoes processing and treatment during
which most of the CO is separated and captured. The flue gas stream with most of the CO
removed is then emitted to the atmosphere. The process is challenging for several reasons.
First, the concentration of CO in the flue gas is relatively low, making separation from
non-condensable gases like nitrogen complex. Second, the overall flue gas exists at a
relatively low pressure (1 bar), meaning a large volume of gas must be treated, resulting in
large equipment size and high energy consumption.

The key advantage of post-combustion technology is that it can be easily retrofitted to
existing equipment and process configurations, making it applicable to power stations, oil
refineries, petrochemical and gas plants, steel and chemical plants--all of which are major
contributors to global CO emissions. Since all these point sources of pollution produce
flue gas at atmospheric pressure with a CO concentration of around 15 percent, the largest
and most immediate impact on global carbon mitigation will likely come from post-
combustion CO capture. Additionally, retrofitting this technology to existing equipment
and process configurations requires little modification, making it easily applicable to the
tail end of the combustion process. Whether we want to separate CO from a dilute or
concentrated flue gas stream, several separation technologies are available. These include
absorption, adsorption, cryogenic distillation, membrane separation, and oxygen chemical
looping.
Absorption refers to the dissolution of CO from the gaseous phase into a liquid phase,
usually a solvent such as an amine. The removal of CO from the solvent is typically
achieved using energy in the form of heat or steam, making absorption an energy-intensive
process. Like absorption, adsorption involves the accumulation of CO on the surface of a
sorbent, which should be highly porous and have a large surface-to-weight ratio, up to 3000
square meters per gram. Materials like activated carbon or Metal Organic Frameworks
(MOFs) can be used, but CO removal in this case also requires various forms of energy,
such as pressure, temperature, or electricity. Pressure swing adsorption is the most widely
used adsorption technology.

Membrane separation involves using filters that allow gases to pass through, directly
separating them. Membranes are efficient at high pressure and low flux and have clear
advantages over both absorption and adsorption because they do not have moving parts
and require minimal energy aside from compensating for pressure loss. However, the main
issue with membrane technology is that it is fragile, difficult to implement on a large scale,
and the selectivity of a membrane decreases as flux increases
Cryogenic distillation, as previously mentioned, involves separating or isolating a gas in
liquid form, which requires liquefaction of the gas at extremely low temperatures. This
method is commonly used for industrial-scale oxygen-nitrogen separation because it allows
for the separation of large volumes of gas. It has also been extensively developed for
separating carbon dioxide from methane mixtures, but like adsorption and absorption, it
can impose a significant energy penalty.

In a chemical looping process, oxygen can be introduced into the reactor or added in the
form of metal oxides rather than in its gaseous form. This approach avoids the need for air
separation units and reduces the energy penalty associated with CO separation. However,
there are significant technical challenges related to this process, particularly in scaling it
up from current lab-scale pilot projects to larger applications.

various gases, including CO. Additionally, CO can be transported by truck and rail,
though these methods are generally used for moving CO from capture sites to nearby
storage locations due to the limitations in distance that trucks can cover effectively. For
longer distances, trucks are not practical. Ship transportation is another viable option,
especially in regions where pipeline infrastructure is limited. Currently, small-scale CO

The transportation of liquefied petroleum gas (LPG) has a well-established history, with
significant expertise developed over a period of 70 years. This expertise has contributed to
LPG becoming a worldwide industry. Similarly, the transport of CO is already taking
place daily in many parts of the world. However, to support large-scale deployment of CO
transportation, particularly for carbon capture, utilization, and storage (CCUS) efforts,
significant investment in transportation infrastructure is essential. Once CO has been
transported to suitable sites, it can be utilized as an alternative feedstock, reducing the
demand for natural resources and their exploitation. For instance, CO can be used in
biological conversion processes to produce fuels and food. It also finds applications in the
food and beverage industry, the manufacturing of plastics, as well as serving as an
extractant and refrigerant. Additionally, CO can be used for enhanced fuel recovery and
in the production of chemicals via electrochemical or thermochemical processes.

CO can be utilized in various chemical processes, most notably in the production of
fertilizers, methanol, and ethanol, all of which have significant industrial value.
Additionally, CO can undergo mineralization, forming stable carbonates. This process is
an effective way to store CO in a solid form, reducing its presence in the atmosphere.CO
also finds applications in fire extinguishers as a fire suppressant, and it can be used as an
inert agent in various industries. In miscellaneous applications, CO can be injected into
metal castings, added to medical oxygen as a respiratory stimulant, or used as an aerosol
propellant. However, among all these options, the most extensive use of CO is in enhanced
oil recovery (EOR). CO-mediated EOR is a technique employed to extract oil from mature
fields--fields that have ceased to be productive through traditional primary and secondary
recovery methods. This process takes advantage of the mutual solubility of crude oil and
CO under the temperature and pressure conditions typical of a geological reservoir. CO,
when injected into the reservoir, dissolves and displaces the remaining oil, allowing for its
recovery, much like a solvent displaces grease from a dirty bicycle chain. This method is
particularly valuable for accessing oil that would otherwise remain untapped in the ground.

In a typical CO flood operation, a pipeline delivers CO to the oil field, where it is directed
into injection wells. As the CO is injected, it moves through the pore spaces in the rocks
and encounters residual crude oil. The crude oil then mixes with the CO, which decreases
the oil's viscosity, pressurizes it, and mobilizes it, ultimately forming a concentrated oil
bank. This concentrated oil bank can then be swept towards the production wells for
extraction. This process enables oil and gas companies to access oil that would otherwise
have been left in the ground. Beyond its use in enhanced oil recovery, CO can also be
stored geologically. Geological storage involves injecting CO into deep underground rock
formations, thereby permanently removing it from the atmosphere. This method of storage
is considered an effective way to mitigate CO emissions and reduce their impact on
climate change.
Geological storage involves injecting CO into deep underground rock formations, thereby
permanently removing it from the atmosphere. There are three types of formations that are
suitable for long-term CO injection, storage, and sequestration. The first and foremost are
depleted oil and gas reservoirs. These are considered the best candidates for CO storage
because they are leak-proof, having sequestered fossil fuels for millions of years. They also
possess high porosity and permeability, making them ideal for CO storage. Additionally,
the oil and gas industry already has extensive knowledge of these reservoirs, including
established injection processes and infrastructure. The second type of suitable formation is
deep saline aquifers, which are saturated with salty formation water or brines that are
unsuitable for drinking purposes. Lastly, deep sedimentary rocks also provide viable
options for CO storage. These formations represent a significant portion of the world's
storage potential, although they have not been as thoroughly characterized or exploited as
oil and gas reservoirs. These deep saline aquifers are saturated with salty formation water
or brines that are unsuitable for drinking. They represent the majority of the global storage
potential for CO, although they have not yet been fully characterized or widely exploited.
When CO is injected into these aquifers, it is slightly more buoyant than the saline water,
allowing both to coexist within the storage formation. Over time, some of the trapped CO
will begin to dissolve into the saline water, becoming trapped indefinitely. Additionally,
another portion of the CO may become trapped in tiny pore spaces, a process known as
residual trapping.
The ultimate trapping process involves the dissolved CO reacting with the reservoir rocks
to form new minerals, a process known as mineral trapping. This can be either relatively
quick or very slow, but it is an effective method for permanently locking CO into a solid
mineral form. Another option for CO storage is unmined coal seams. These seams contain
fractures that significantly improve the permeability of the medium, allowing CO to
migrate through the coal and be physically absorbed by the coal's micropores. Typically,
carbon dioxide is stored underground in a supercritical fluid state, where it exhibits
properties of both a gas and a liquid, enabling more efficient storage.
So, supercritical CO means that the CO is at a temperature above 31.1 degrees Celsius
and at a pressure above 72.9 atmospheres. At these conditions, CO reaches its critical
points. In this state, the CO exhibits properties of both a gas and a liquid. It is dense like a
liquid but flows like a gas, making it particularly advantageous for storage as it requires
substantially less volume than if it were at standard room temperature and pressure
conditions.

In particular, supercritical CO is dense like a liquid but has the viscosity of a gas. The
main advantage of storing CO in this supercritical state is that it requires substantially less
storage volume compared to storing it at standard room temperature and pressure
conditions. Additionally, as you go deeper into the Earth's crust, both the temperature and
the pressure of fluids naturally increase. At depths below approximately 800 meters, the
temperature and fluid pressures typically exceed the critical point of CO in most locations
on Earth, ensuring that the CO remains in its supercritical condition.

So, this means that CO injected at this depth or deeper will remain in this supercritical
condition given the temperature and pressures present. Typically, the geological
characteristics of the storage sites should include rock formations with enough millimeter-
sized voids or pores to provide the capacity to store CO. Additionally, the pores in the
rocks should be sufficiently connected, a feature known as permeability, to accept the
amount of CO at the rate at which it is injected. This permeability allows the CO to move
and spread out within the formation.

And there should also be a seal rock, cap rock, or barrier at the top of the formation to
contain the CO permanently. There are four distinct mechanisms that trap the injected CO
in the subsurface. First and foremost is structural and stratigraphic trapping. Structural
trapping is the physical trapping of CO in the rocks and traps the maximum amount of
CO.

Next, we have residual trapping. Residual trapping refers to trapping CO in the pore spaces
between rocks, where the rocks act like a rigid sponge. Then, there is solubility trapping.
Solubility trapping occurs when CO interacts with a brine solution. At the CO-brine water
interface, some CO molecules dissolve into the brine water within the rock's pore space.

And then we have mineral trapping. Mineral trapping refers to the reaction that occurs
when CO, dissolved in the brine water within the rocks, reacts with the minerals in the
rock. When CO dissolves in water, it forms a weak carbonic acid and eventually
bicarbonate. Over extended periods, this weak acid can react with minerals in the
surrounding rock to form solid carbonate minerals. In this way, we can permanently trap
and store the portion of injected CO. In the long term, the dissolved CO will eventually
mineralize into carbonate, increasing storage security.

Apart from geological storage, we can also deliberately inject CO into the ocean at great
depths, where most of it would remain isolated from the atmosphere for prolonged periods,
possibly for several centuries. CO can be transported via pipelines or with the help of
ships. Various technologies have been considered to enable and increase ocean storage.
One option is to place CO on a ship and then directly inject it into the ocean or deposit it
on the ocean floor. CO loaded on a ship can either be dispersed from a tote pipe or
transported to fixed platforms feeding a CO lake on the ocean floor.

Now, such CO lakes must be at a depth greater than 3 kilometers, where CO is denser
than seawater. Any of these approaches could, in principle, be used in conjunction with
neutralization with carbonate minerals. Apart from mitigation options, we also have
geoengineering strategies. Geoengineering is the deliberate large-scale intervention in the
Earth's natural system to counteract climate change. It involves the large-scale engineering
and manipulation of the planetary environment to monitor or counteract global climate
change in response to the emission of greenhouse gases.
There is a wide range of proposed geoengineering techniques, broadly classified into two
groups. One is solar radiation management, or solar engineering, which aims to reflect a
small portion of the incoming solar radiation back into space. This approach helps
counteract the temperature rise caused by increased levels of greenhouse gases in the
atmosphere, which otherwise absorb energy and raise temperature.

The other option is carbon management, which aims to remove carbon dioxide or other
greenhouse gases from the atmosphere. This approach directly counters the increased
greenhouse effect and ocean acidification. These techniques must be implemented on a
global scale to significantly impact greenhouse gas levels in the atmosphere.

When we look into solar radiation management options, one of the methods is albedo
enhancement. This can be achieved by increasing the reflectiveness of clouds or the land
surface, thereby reflecting more of the sun's heat back into space. This method ensures that
more of the sun's heat is reflected back into space. Another option involves using space
reflectors, which can block a small proportion of sunlight before it reaches the earth.
Additionally, stratospheric aerosols can be introduced, which are small reflective particles
in the upper atmosphere designed to reflect some sunlight before it reaches the earth's
surface.

Regarding carbon management, afforestation is a viable option. This involves engaging in
global-scale tree-planting efforts to absorb CO from the atmosphere. we can engage in
global-scale tree-planting efforts to absorb CO from the atmosphere. Another approach is
direct air capture, which involves building large machines that can remove carbon dioxide
directly from the ambient air and store it elsewhere. Additionally, ocean fertilization can
be employed by adding nutrients to the oceans in selected locations to increase primary
production, which draws down carbon dioxide from the atmosphere.

And then we also have enhanced weathering. Enhanced weathering involves exposing
large quantities of minerals that will then react with carbon dioxide in the atmosphere and
store the resulting compound in the ocean or soil. When we talk about solar radiation
management, we need to understand that it has its own pros and cons. For example, some
of the approaches to limit incoming solar radiation can be relatively inexpensive to
implement but must be sustainable over time to produce meaningful effects. Additionally,
other approaches to limiting global warming resemble science fiction.

For example, putting a solar screen in space would involve tremendous expense and require
precise navigation and engineering. Applying suggested approaches mainly to moderate
impact hotspots, such as the Arctic, might be possible. However, when we look into the
cons, we need to understand that geoengineering is only treating the symptoms and not the
cause. Geoengineering might give us an excuse not to reduce greenhouse gas emissions
and can only address some aspects of the problem. It mitigates the rise of global mean
temperature but is definitely not a cure for ocean acidification. This type of geoengineering
cannot substitute for greenhouse gas emission reduction. A very high rate of warming in
the case of failure of implemented geoengineering solutions could lead to even greater
chances of global warming. Unintended consequences also raise questions about
responsibility and cost. Moreover, several ethical and political questions are associated
with the large-scale implementation of geoengineering techniques.
