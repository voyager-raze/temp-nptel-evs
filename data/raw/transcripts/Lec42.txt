               Sources of MSW, MSW Generation Rates and Composition
Welcome, everyone to the second lecture in week 9.

Today, we are going to look at the sources of MSW, MSW generation rates and their
composition.
So, as I said the concepts that we are going to cover in this particular lecture are the sources
of MSW, what are the activities that are associated with municipal solid waste management,
what are the MSW generation rates total as well as per capita, what are the factors affecting
these generation rates and composition.

These are some of the keywords.
So, let us start with sources and types of municipal solid waste. The very first one is
residential. So, all residential buildings single family as well as multifamily dwellings, they
are going to generate food waste, paper, cardboard, plastic, textile, leather, yard, glass, metals,
ash, all kinds of household goods which eventually end up in the waste bin are part of this
residential source. Similarly, from commercial and industrial sources you will have similar
waste as part of their housekeeping waste and the only thing that is less in commercial and
industrial waste is that food waste is less, but you will have construction and demolition
material, you may have hazardous material coming from industrial processes and special
waste associated with the industrial process. Similarly, for the commercial activities. Now
commercial activities include stores, hotels, restaurants, markets, office buildings and so on.
We also have institutional sources, schools, hospitals, prisons, any institutions, they are
similar to the commercial activities and they will generate similar types of municipal solid
waste. We also have construction and demolition activities. So, in any part of the towns or
cities you will find that either there is construction of new buildings or roads and so on or
there is demolition of old structures. So, in all cases you have wood, steel, concrete, dirt and
so on that is part of the waste generated. Municipal services include street cleaning,
landscaping, beaches, water and wastewater treatment plants and all waste generated by
these activities will become part of MSW. The next one is also process manufacturing and that
again is similar to industrial processes. Very few cities, the larger cities generally do not have
agriculture, but some of the medium towns, medium sized towns and smaller towns may have
agricultural activity within the city. In those cases, you may have crops orchards, vineyards,
dairies, farms and so on and that agricultural waste may also end up in the municipal solid
waste.
This is a flow chart that shows the processes associated with municipal solid waste
management. We are all generators of solid waste. If you think about any of the things that
you do, whatever you do in your home, you have a trash can where you put in all your solid
materials. Some of it is biodegradable, some of it is non-biodegradable and some of it is
recyclable. So paper, plastic, metal, glass, electrical and electronic waste, all of that most of
us are separating it at source or later. So, separation means sorting the material. I separate
my paper from my plastic and metal and so on. So, each one is separated and stored
separately so that we can provide it to the recyclist, either they come to our door or we take
it to different collection centres and so on. That is the most common scenario in our country.
The activities that are shown in red, so one collection, two transfer and transport and three
transformation or processing of waste including final disposal. So, these four steps are the
purview, the responsibility of the local municipality or administrative authority. So, the local
municipality or municipal administration has to collect waste from the individual generators.
So, they have to collect waste from the residential, commercial, industrial areas. They have to
transfer and transport it either to a central facility or take it for disposal. There was a time
and perhaps it is still the case in certain cities and towns that we have landfills where this
material is put in directly and whatever can be recycled is taken for recycling. What is
becoming more common is that the waste is collected door to door that is part of the current
set of rules and regulations. It is collected from door to door. It is taken to a central facility
where it is processed either manually segregated or mechanically segregated. The different
streams of waste are then taken for recycling. They are sold to the recyclers and whatever is
remaining goes for disposal. Disposal there are two options, landfilling and incineration. We
will be looking at those options later.
Let us take a global perspective about municipal solid waste generation. It is an urban
phenomenon. First of all, whatever we are looking at in this particular week is related to
urban centres only. We are not looking at rural areas. It is one of the most expensive essential
municipal services that have to be provided by the urban local bodies. So, whether it is the
local administration, the municipality or the municipal corporation, this is one of their
responsibilities. In low-income countries, the municipal solid waste management budget can
be more than 19% of the total budget. As the income level increases, the percentage required
for providing the same services or even better-quality services goes down. So high income
countries are spending around 4% of their total municipal budget on waste management and
middle-income countries are spending around 11% of their total budget. The total amount
of MSW that was generated globally in 2018 or perhaps a year before that is 2 billion tonnes
per year and approximately one third has not been managed properly. High income countries
have only 16% of the world population, but they are generating 34% of the total global waste.
So, the first thing to understand is that the level of income, whether you measure it in terms
of gross domestic product or gross national product, whatever the measure of income is, it is
related to the amount of waste generated. We will be going through that more later. Per capita
waste generation ranges from 0.1 kilogram per capita per day in sub-Saharan Africa all the
way to 4.54 kilograms of per capita per day in North America and the top three countries in
North America are Bermuda, Canada and the US in terms of per capita waste generation rates.
The global average is 0.4 kilograms per capita per day and the range is 0.46 to 2.2 kilograms
per capita per day.
What are the factors that affect municipal solid waste generation rates and their composition?
The first thing is population size and the simplest example is shown here. You have the
population for 23 metros has been plotted in this graph as a function of population and total
waste generated. So as the population increases in a particular city, the amount of waste is
also going to increase. You can see the R square value is very high and that means that
population is directly related to the total amount of waste generated. If I divide the total
amount of waste generated by the population, I get what is called per capita waste generation
rate. So for the same cities, if I plot per capita waste generation against population, I find that
there is no relationship between these two parameters. The R square value is very small
which means there is no relation. We do not have data for India, so I have used data here for
the US and you have total solid waste generation for the US from 1960 to about 2018. The
blue line is total MSW generation and you can see how much it has grown from close to 100
million tons to 300 million tons. Per capita waste generation you can see has behaved in a
slightly different manner. From 1960 to 1990, there has been a steady increase in per capita
waste generation rate. It has saturated or rather plateaued after that. So, from 1990 to 2018,
you see very small change in the per capita waste generation rate. It is currently around 5
pounds per person per day or per capita per day and that is a little bit, that is somewhat close
to 2.5 kilograms per capita per day. In India for comparison, we are around 0.5 to 0.6
kilograms per capita per day in India at present. So that is the first factor.
Let us come to the second factor, seasonal variations. Because of change in seasons, you will
have different amounts of leaf fall, the street sweeping, the yard waste will change. In our
country, in the monsoon versus the dry season, during the monsoon if the community bin is
open to the atmosphere, the rainwater comes into the bin and that increases the weight of
the waste generated. So these factors contribute to variations in the amount of waste
generated due to indifferent seasons. Then we have transient populations at certain locations
can result in significant differences during different seasons. So, tourist and pilgrimage
locations, they have a peak season. Peak season can be 10 times higher than what is the low
season. So peak season when the tourists or the pilgrims come in depending on how much
influx there is will result in proportionate waste generation. And in the low season, you have
only the residential population, the permanently residential population and that is your
background level during the low season. Even in educational institutions like ours, we have a
residential educational institution where we have a vacation period and the academic session
period. So, three to four months in a year, the students go home and during that time, the
population on campus is much less compared to the rest of the year. So, during that period,
you will have less waste generation as well. If the area of interest, whether it is a city, town,
residential, campus, whatever it is, if they have recycling programs, then part of the waste is
being diverted to those recycling programs. So, the amount of waste that has to be taken to a
landfill or to an incinerator will be much less. So that will also impact the total amount of
waste generated. Then we come to location in terms of geographic conditions. So those
geographic conditions will have any difference in geographic conditions will be due to
climatic variations. So, for example, a tropical country or a temperate country, they will have
different amounts of waste generation, partly because of the nature of the vegetation in those
areas and partly because of, like I said, the monsoon season and so on. So that contributes to
the amount of waste generated and the weight of the waste generated, that is a major factor.
Then we come to infrastructure development. How much of the area in a city is paved versus
green? So paved areas will have less vegetation related waste. So, you will not have so much
garden waste, you will not have leaf litter and so on. But in green areas, you will have all of
that. So that will also have an impact on the total amount of waste generated. Then we have
socio political conditions. As you know, right now we have the Swachh Bharat mission that is
in place, it has been in place for quite some time. And that is an example of a change in
condition. So, this is where the government and the political infrastructure along with this
particular mission is going to encourage society as a whole to take proactive measures for
managing municipal solid waste. So, citizens are being encouraged to take part in various
programs. We have door to door collection; we have segregation of waste in several towns
and cities. So, these are all part of the government mission to achieve certain objectives. We
then come to number of persons per dwelling.
