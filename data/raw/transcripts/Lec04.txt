So we now come to what is called the capital stock model. This is the model that is used
by the World Bank, to explain the nature of capital, stock, and sustainability. It's based on
a very simple premise, that if we live off the interest and not the capital, then the basis of
prosperity is maintained. The analogy here is with your account, let's say in a bank. If
you have a certain capital that you deposit in the bank and you live off of the interest,
then you will have a regular income for the long term.

But if you withdraw part of that capital, then the basis of your income for the future is
endangered. So the analogy here is that the capital is our natural resources. This is our
natural capital and this natural capital should not be the basis of our prosperity. Instead,
we should be living off of the interest or the surplus that we gain from this capital
because if we deplete the capital, then the long-term existence of human beings is
basically endangered. So our future generations are likely to face a deficit and they
already are in many parts of the world, including our own country, we are already facing
a deficit in terms of natural capital.

So we'll be talking about that later in the next lecture. And this is the basis, this is the
premise on which the entire idea of capital stock model is based on. So, the capital stock
for sustainable development is the sum of the capital stock for the environment, the sum
of the capital stock for the economy, and some of the capital stocks for the social
environment or society at large.

We then come to how can we measure environmental impacts. Now this word ecological
footprint has been used very frequently in media and in technical publications and so on.
So, we need to understand what it means and how can we quantify environmental
impacts using this concept. This concept is based on a couple of fundamentals.
Everything that we use, food, clothes, housing, anything that you can think of is coming
from nature. So, everything that we are using comes from nature. When you think of the
ecological pyramid, the lowest layer, the lowest trophic level is occupied by primary
producers.
These primary producers are responsible for our food, our clothes and even our housing
material. So, primary productivity is the foundation on which our standard of living is
based on. So, whatever we take from nature is based on the primary productivity and we
are utilizing it. That is our resource consumption. So, we are extracting natural capital or
natural resources.
We are using it for many different activities in our daily lives and in the process we are
generating huge amounts of waste. Some of this waste is thrown back to nature. As long
as nature can absorb this waste, assimilate it, absorb it without losing anything
permanently, it is considered to be within the assimilative capacity of nature. There is no
problem as long as that happens. However, what is happening and I will spend a lot more
time on that is that the waste we are generating is exceeding the assimilative capacity of
nature to absorb it and that is our, this is the concept for quantifying the ecological
footprint.
So are we utilizing resources that can be replenished within let's say a short amount of
time or are they permanently eliminated and so on? What is the long-term impact? So
that is the underlying basis of the ecological footprint and we will be going into details in
the next few slides.
