So, let us start with the National Green Tribunal. The National Green Tribunal was
established on 18th of October 2010 under the National Green Tribunal Act. The goal of
this particular organization is to provide effective and expeditious disposal of cases
relating to environmental protection and conservation of forest and other natural
resources. This includes the enforcement of any legal right relating to the environment
and providing relief and compensation for damages to persons, property and for matters
connected to that. It is a specialized body that is equipped with the necessary expertise. It
is not bound by the code of civil procedure, but it is guided by the principles of natural
justice. The tribunal tries to dispose of applications or appeals within months of their
being filed. The principal place of sitting of the tribunal is New Delhi. There are four
other locations in the country where the NGT sits and that is Bhopal, Pune, Kolkata, and
Chennai.

We now come to the next major topic for this lecture and that is the water quality index.
Now, water quality indices are used by different groups, different countries for providing
a uniform scale of comparison of water quality. The advantage of using a single number,
water quality index means it is a single number that represents water quality. We have
already seen that water quality cannot be measured in one way only. We have far more
than 10 parameters. I have covered only the 10 most important parameters in previous
lectures, but we know they are not adequate.

So, because we do not have a single measure that can be used to represent water quality,
this index is proposed by different groups, research groups in different countries and
there are so many indices that are available in the literature, but we are going to stick to
one of the earliest ones. So, coming back to this point, water quality indices provide a
uniform scale of comparison for water quality in any part of the world with a single
number value because it is difficult to compare so many different parameters for different
water sources at the same time. Now, when you have a water quality index, it is a single
value. You can compare across both time and space. So, I can look at a particular river,
and I can say 10 years ago the water quality index was 100, and today it is 50. So, that I
am comparing temporally. So, that is temporal variation. I can also say this river at its
upstream end has a water quality index of 70 and at its downstream end it has come down
to 20. So, that is a comparison of the spatial change in water quality and this happens all
the time. Rivers, streams, they have severe changes happen in their water quality both
over time and space.

So, this helps to compare it on a single uniform scale. As I said, there are several water
quality indices that are available in the literature. The earliest publication is by Horton in

So, we now come to the last part of this week as well as this particular topic and that is
reuse and recycling of waste water. It is very important for everyone to realize that our
goal today in today's world is zero discharge of wastewater. We want to ensure that no
wastewater is discharged to any surface water body or even groundwater. Now, how to
achieve such a tremendous goal? So zero discharge goal can be achieved only if
conventional and advanced wastewater treatment is done. So that means it requires a lot
of effort, additional effort may be required.

This treated waste water effluent can be used for many different purposes. The most
common, the oldest use of waste water traditionally it has been used for irrigation or
horticulture. So, if you are using wastewater for irrigation or horticulture that is after
conventional treatment, I will come to more details in the next few slides. It can also be
used for aquaculture and eco-restoration. It can be used for groundwater recharge and
what is becoming increasingly evident is that in water-scarce regions people are realizing
that waste water can be treated to the level of drinking water and it can be used as such.
The best documented cases of wastewater to drinking water are in Namibia since 1968
and in Singapore since 1998.
So what I have over here on this slide is a very simple schematic that explains waste
water treatment. So, what we are going to look at is a very quick review of how we treat
wastewater. So, let us say we have municipal wastewater. It contains large numbers of
different types of solids. You will have plastic bags, you will have branches, twigs,
leaves, stones, pebbles, all kinds of things end up in municipal sewage. So, our first
objective is primary waste water treatment which will remove these objectionable solids.
And we use screening, we use grid chambers, all these kinds of processes are used for
removing these objectionable solids. Then we come to secondary wastewater treatment.
So after these solids have been removed, we are now left with dissolved biodegradable
material and that is represented by BOD. So our second objective or rather secondary
wastewater treatment objective is removal of BOD, and that is done by biological
processes. So we have activated sludge process, we have trickling filters, we have so
many other types of biological processes that are used at the secondary stage. Primary
plus secondary is considered as conventional wastewater treatment. After conventional
wastewater treatment we disinfect the wastewater prior to discharge. Why is it
disinfected? Because here in the secondary stage, we have encouraged biological
processes which means the number the concentration of microbial organisms including
bacteria is very very high and there may be pathogenic bacteria present in this water.

So, to ensure that there is no possibility of pathogens being present in the effluent of the
secondary wastewater treatment process we disinfect it prior to discharge. The
disinfection can be either ozonation, UV treatment, so many other possibilities exist.
Now, this waste water when it is used or reused it can be reused for irrigation, and it is
generally used for irrigation of non-food crops because we still do not want any bacteria
to be present in the effluent that is in contact with food crops that is one part. Now, this
effluent also contains high levels of nitrogen and phosphorous. If this water is discharged
into any receiving water body it will cause eutrophication in that water body and
eutrophication is again degradation of water quality which is not acceptable. So it is
much better to treat it further and use it for either irrigation of food crops directly. That
means at the secondary stage it can be used after further treatment for food crop irrigation
or it can be used for other purposes and I will come to that in the next slides. Where there
is a possibility of using wastewater for levels maybe to the level of drinking water or
even other uses we may have to go for higher levels of wastewater treatment. So, we need
to remove contaminants like heavy metals, pesticides, micropollutants and so on. So, I
will be talking about that in the next few slides.

How do we apply the wastewater for irrigation to land areas? So we have the most
common methods are the first two methods. We have surface irrigation or overland flow.
You can have your ridge and furrow irrigation where you have a higher part where the
plant is sown and a lower part where the water is allowed to flow. So that is called ridge
and furrow irrigation or sheet flow and in that case, the land has to have a gentle slope,

growth and you will also get generation of oxygen again in the root zone for the aerobic
decomposition of organic matter that is present in wastewater. So that is how the
constructed wetland function where the oxygen in the root zone is generated and the
microbes are going to utilize that oxygen and decompose the organic matter. Nutrients
from the wastewater are utilized by the plants and microbes for their growth. The plants
will need to be harvested periodically.
