So, some of the concepts that we are going to go through are treatment processes and

The keywords for this particular lecture are landfilling, biodegradable, compost,

So, before we go into any details, let us look at the main goal today in solid waste
management or rather municipal solid waste management. Our current goal is zero waste
to landfills. So, remember the ISWM objectives, one of our goals was to minimize
landfilling. We want to do away with landfills.

So, landfilling is no longer an acceptable option, but because it is the only option in some
cases, so it cannot be completely eliminated. But it is important to remember that many
European countries have already achieved less than 1% waste to landfills. So, we will be
going through all these points as we go through this lecture. We will start first with
treatment options. What are our common treatment options? The first is composting, and
I think most of you are familiar with composting today.

Composting is where you take bacteria and fungi that are present in soil or in wastewater
and you add them to your waste content to the organic fraction of your waste and allow it
to be degraded over a fairly long period of time. The minimum time required is one
month and it can go up to three months or even six months. You can do the same thing
without bacteria and fungi. You can use earthworms and if you are using earthworms, it
is called vermicomposting. You can have anaerobic digestion in which case the benefit is
that you get biogas and I have already mentioned that biogas is the combination of CO2
and methane and you need to separate the two gases to get a useful fuel. You can
pelletize your fuel. The solid waste material which is wet can be ground to a very fine
material. It can be pelletized; small pellets can be created to form what is called refuse
derived fuel and these pellets can be sold in the market as fuel.
Then we have disposal options. We have two main options. One is incineration of the
waste followed by landfilling or directly you can go for landfilling and like I said
landfilling which used to be a very popular option in developed countries especially those
with large amounts of land like Australia, Canada, they have huge amounts of land and
landfilling was done at a fairly inexpensive level. However, with current understanding,
we do not want to pollute the soil, we do not want to use the land for dumping waste. So
our basic goal, we are back to our basic goal that we do not want any waste to go to
landfills.

So, let us take a look at the global status. What is the most popular method? Composting
is fairly popular. It is 5.5%, and it is followed by incineration at 11%, controlled

is so let me back up here. Controlled landfilling is 4%, sanitary landfilling is 7.7%, open

a combination of open dumping and unsanitary landfilling. So, that is 25%.
These are also these choices of disposal methods. I said this in an earlier lecture that one
of the factors that determines a country's choice in terms of treatment and disposal
method is the availability of land and the cost of incineration. Now remember that
incineration is expensive as well for the simple reason that you need air pollution control
equipment and because this air pollution control equipment is fairly expensive therefore
incineration becomes an expensive option. So, given the fact that cost and land

So let us take a look at the low-income countries. In low-income countries 93% is openly

of waste by anywhere from 60 to let us say 90% because you are left only with the ash.
The greater the organic content it all depends on the organic content of the waste. So if

countries quite obviously and as the income level increases open dumping of waste

So what are we doing in India? Urban local bodies meaning municipal corporations,
municipalities, administrative authorities, they generally go for one of these four options
that you see in this graph. So, by far most of them are going for composting. Composting
at the large scale or rather full-scale plants is highly successful. It has been found to be
low maintenance, low cost, it is easy to install, there is some amount of capital
expenditure, there is some amount of land requirement, but it requires low-skilled
operators, any person even without an education can be trained to run a composting plant.
It is not difficult at all. So that is one point in its favor. Depending on the land area
requirement and the area that you are setting up the plant in, that land cost will vary
obviously and therefore that will contribute to the cost of composting but otherwise it has
proved to be the best possible option all over the country. So we have more than 1000
plants, out of that 983 were operational in 2016-17 and I am sure many more have been
set up since then, and there are several more that were in the planning stage at that time.
Vermicomposting which is composting done by earthworms is also a highly popular
process. What is the reason for not being as popular as regular composting? As I
mentioned before regular composting is relying on bacteria and fungi to break down the
biodegradable fraction of MSW, convert it to a stable compost material which has high
nutrient content and then use it as a supplemental fertilizer. As a supplement to fertilizers
in fields and gardens and so on. Vermicomposting results in a compost that is higher
quality compared to the regular compost. But you are breeding earthworms, and not all
earthworms are equally effective that is one issue and the second issue is in fact to
complete that idea the most effective earthworms are imported into India from Africa.
The Indian earthworms for some reason are not as efficient as the African earthworm. So
that is one issue. The second issue is that breeding earthworms is not so easy.
Earthworms are extremely sensitive organisms. They require high levels of moisture.
They should not be exposed to sunlight. So, composting which can be done under
literally under the sky cannot be done with vermicomposting.

For vermicomposting, you need a closed enclosure. So the compost beds have to be
covered throughout, and you need to maintain moisture at fairly high levels. So unless all
those conditions are met vermicomposting is not effective. But the end product is very
high-quality compost. I have also I should mention over here that composting plants
which have been created under basically under a shed have proved to be even more
successful than the ones that are in the open. So having an enclosure where you have a
cover or some kind of roof over the compost enclosure is definitely improving the
efficiency of the composting process. So in all cases, having an enclosure is worth the
cost.

Then we come to anaerobic digestion. Anaerobic digestion has a major end product and
that is biogas. It is a fuel and it can be useful. However, operation and maintenance of
biogas plants has proved to be a challenge all over the country. There are very few plants
that have been able to operate for more than 5 years and getting skilled operators to
maintain these plants has proved to be a major challenge. Then we come to refuse derived
fuel based plants. Refuse-derived fuel plants are technically feasible. It is possible to
pelletize the organic fractions of MSW and sell them as fuel and it is much easier.
However, this has also not proved to be a popular option for many reasons these pellets
of fuel are not acceptable to the poor who are considered to be the target consumer group.
They are not willing to accept it for the simple reason that air pollution is out of control if
you were to use RDF as a fuel. So the air pollution problems generated by RDF unless it
is burned in a controlled environment is not happening in our country, and therefore there
are no takers for this material in most cases.
Then we come to waste to energy conversion. Now I mentioned in the very first lecture in
this particular week that waste to energy conversion itself requires inputs of energy
because first thing you need to do is segregate the organic fraction from the inorganic
material. And then you can derive energy from whatever material is left in the organic
fraction and you can derive energy in two different methods. In fact, I have put it as
chemical processing and biological processing. The chemical processing is also called
thermal processing. So you can burn the waste within combustion we have two types of
combustion we have stoichiometric combustion and excess air combustion. If you
remember the ThOD calculations that we did, you can do something very similar with
combustion of the waste. So let us say you know the empirical chemical formula for the
waste you can determine what amount of oxygen is required to get complete oxidation of
the waste. So let us say you have waste with carbon, hydrogen and oxygen we will ignore
nitrogen, phosphorus, sulfur and all of that. So, just keeping carbon, hydrogen and
oxygen you can write a reaction and take it to CO2 and water. So that would be that will
give you an estimate of the oxygen required to completely oxidize the waste. So that is
called stoichiometric combustion. We know that if you provide just that amount of
oxygen you are not going to get 100% oxidation because of the inefficiencies of the
process. So what is generally done is excess air combustion. So combustion can be
stoichiometric or excess air combustion. Those are our first two options. The second
option is gasification. Gasification is done under sub-oxic conditions. So if you have your
waste, you know the stoichiometric requirement, but you provide oxygen that is less than
the stoichiometric requirement which means you will have partially reduced, reducing
conditions instead of completely oxidizing conditions.
So in that case let us come to this slide. You will have sub-stoichiometric amounts of air
supplied. You will get endothermic and exothermic reactions which are self-sustaining
and because you have a reduced partially reducing environment, you will get some
reduced compounds. So you will get hydrogen gas, methane, carbon monoxide and
carbon dioxide. This together is called synthetic gas or SYN gas, SYN gas is the short
form.

If you burn the material without air that is called pyrolysis. These are endothermic
reactions. An external source of energy is required. You will get two outputs SYN gas or
synthetic gas and pyrolytic oil, and if you are able to capture both these types of outputs
and sell them as fuel, then this becomes cost-effective. I will also mention about
combustion. Combustion is exothermic, no external energy source is required. Energy
recovery is possible in both combustion as well as gasification. Our end products are
completely oxidized compounds, unlike the other two processes. So, in the combustion
process, we have CO2, nitrogen, SO2 and water. These are the types of reactors that can
be used, incinerators or reactors that can be used for burning the material. So we have
mass-fired incinerators, refuse-derived fuel incinerators, fluidized bed incinerators or
combustors. Vertical fixed bed, horizontal fixed bed, multiple hearth, rotary kiln,
fluidized bed, all these are gasification reactors and then we have fluidized bed reactors
for pyrolysis.
Coming back to this slide we now will take a look at biological processing. Because of
lack of time, I am not going to go into any details. I have already mentioned that
composting is very popular. There are two types of composting that are done in India. We
have regular composting which relies on bacteria, fungi, and some other higher
organisms but mainly bacteria and fungi and we have vermicomposting which relies on
earthworms. They are both under aerobic conditions. So, both these processes are done
under aerobic conditions. So aerobic composting is done, and most of the composting
processes are really, they are started under aerobic conditions, and then depending on
how often you mix the composting material they can be either aerobic or anaerobic
because the waste will eventually become anaerobic if it is not mixed too often. And
there are several factors which we do not have time to go into, but it is important to
remember that it starts as an aerobic process, and depending on the amount of mixing or
turning that is done it may become anaerobic or it may remain aerobic. Then we come to
anaerobic digestion, and that is biogas generation. Again, I am not going to go into any
details because again this is a very complex process and the only thing I am going to say
here is that no air is provided, in fact, air is removed, and this entire process will result in
the fermentation of the organic material to result in an end product which is biogas. So
you have CO2 and methane that are produced.

Other types of materials for example agricultural waste, sugarcane, what else, Jatrufa in
India, these are crops that can be converted to ethanol, and ethanol has been used as a
substitute for petrol or gasoline. So that is another form of biofuel that can be derived
from agricultural waste and so on. So that is another option.
Now given that we have at least four options in our country how do we decide which one
to choose? So, I have made the process quite simple over here based on only two
parameters, one is moisture content, and the other is organic content. If you know and
you know how simple it is to measure these two parameters, moisture content and
organic content for any waste can be measured quite easily. If you have a waste that has
high moisture content, meaning lots of water and high organic content it has been
segregated. So, the organic fraction has been segregated from the inorganic material and
with food waste you can do that, with agricultural waste you can do that and so on. So
high moisture content high organic content is best suited for anaerobic digestion because
you will get biogas or biofuels from that. If you have high moisture content and low
organic content, now Indian waste in general has a fairly high moisture content. It is
basically food waste, and it does not have too much dry organic waste. So, because it has
high moisture content, it is suitable for composting and why low organic content? I have
already shown you results for Kharagpur and in the previous lecture I showed you that
the organic content is not very high, it is about 20%. So, if the percentage of organic
content is not very high, the moisture content is sufficient for composting then that is the
ideal option, and you will find that that is another reason why most municipalities have
gone for composting. It has proved to be effective for Indian waste.

If the material is low in moisture content and low in organic content which means soil
dirt, so when you segregate your waste let us say I am segregating my waste these days
into biodegradable material which is the food waste and the kitchen waste and in non-
biodegradable material. That non-biodegradable material is not fit for these two
processes. So composting and biogas cannot be used because it is non-biodegradable.
Can you burn it? The organic content is not sufficient for burning. So you are left with
only one option, and that is landfilling. And then we come to the last option: low
moisture content, high organic content. If you want to burn any material it should not
have any moisture. You cannot have anything that is zero moisture. There is always about
10% moisture, just a rough average. Even newspaper has about 10% moisture content,
even dry newspaper. It can be burned. We all know that it burns very well and it will give
you heat which can then be converted into electricity and so on. So, if you have waste
material with low moisture content high organic content, it is best suited for incineration
which can then be, the heat can then be utilized for generating electricity. Or it can be
used directly. So, some of the thumb rules are also noted here. The minimum organic
content for compost should be 12 to 18% by wet weight. You can see it is at the lower
end. So high organic content is for anaerobic digestion and for compost it is 12 to 18%,
so it is at the lower end. Moisture content should be 15 to 25%. I do not have the compost
quality standards here, but you can refer to them. They are available on the internet.
Combustion and biofuel generation, minimum organic content has to be 25 to 50%. So
that is at the higher end. This is the bottom row of the table. You also have to meet some
other criteria for combustion. Moisture content should be less than 50%. Minimum
calorific value should be less than 1790 kilocalories per kilogram. And I have shown you
in the previous slide that the average Indian municipal solid waste does not have enough
calorific value. So what we have seen in full-scale plants in India is that they are
insufficient and not sustainable in the long term. They also need to have high capacity. So
you need economies of scale to make it sustainable financially in the long term. So, you
need a minimum plant capacity of 300 tons per day. Biofuel can be generated only when
the solids concentration is anywhere between 5 to 20%. So, 5 to 25% can result in high
amounts of biofuel generation, and this means the moisture content has to be less than
95%.
