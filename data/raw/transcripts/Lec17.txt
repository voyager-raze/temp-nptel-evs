By the end of this lecture, you should have a fundamental understanding of the following
terminologies: climate forcings, global warming potential, carbon sources, carbon sinks,
and the Keeling Curve. You should also be able to explain what the Keeling Curve indicates
about atmospheric carbon dioxide levels. As I mentioned in the last lecture, the warming
of the Earth's climate system is unequivocal, and many observed changes in the Earth's
climate are consistent with rising temperatures. A common question that arises is whether
these changes are occurring due to differences in solar radiation. While studies have shown
that solar variability has played a role in determining the Earth's climate in the distant past,
multiple lines of evidence indicate that the current global warming cannot be attributed to
changes in energy from the sun.

In fact, if you closely examine this graph, you'll notice that since 1750, the average amount
of energy we have received from the sun has either remained constant or increased slightly.
If a more active sun were responsible for the warming, we would expect to see a uniform
increase in temperature across all layers of the atmosphere. However, what we are actually
observing is warming in the lower parts of the atmosphere, specifically the troposphere--
the lowermost layer of Earth's atmosphere--while the upper atmosphere is cooling. This
phenomenon occurs because greenhouse gases are slowing the loss of heat from the lower
atmosphere.
Given this, what then is driving climate change? Based on a strong and credible body of
evidence, most climate scientists have concluded that the observed increase in global
average surface temperature since the mid-20th century is likely due to the increase in
anthropogenic greenhouse gas concentrations, such as carbon dioxide, methane, and
nitrous oxide.

Among these greenhouse gases, the most significant contributor to global warming is
carbon dioxide. As you can see, its atmospheric concentration has increased from about
290 parts per million in 1880 to more than 400 parts per million in 2013. This marked the
first time in human history that atmospheric carbon dioxide concentrations surpassed such
high levels, and it has been steadily rising since. In fact, daily records of atmospheric CO
concentrations, measured at the Mauna Loa Observatory on the island of Hawaii, as well
as in the Arctic, show a consistent upward trend.

Measurements taken since 1958, particularly from the Mauna Loa Observatory in Hawaii
and the South Pole, demonstrate this steady annual increase in atmospheric CO
concentrations. These measurements are made in remote locations like the Mauna Loa
Observatory and the South Pole because these areas are not significantly influenced by
local processes such as industrialization and transportation, allowing for more accurate
global readings.
Measurements made at such remote locations are essentially representative of the
background atmosphere. This record of maintaining CO concentration is known as the
Keeling Curve. The Keeling Curve is a graph that represents the concentration of
atmospheric carbon dioxide since 1958, named after its creator, Dr. Charles David Keeling.
Dr. Keeling, who was associated with the Scripps Institution of Oceanography at the
University of California, San Diego, began studying atmospheric carbon dioxide in 1956
by regularly taking air samples and measuring the amount of CO in the Earth's atmosphere.
Over time, he noticed a distinct pattern. He observed a sawtooth-like pattern in the data,
where the air samples taken at night contained higher concentrations of CO. He inferred
that the higher CO concentrations in the samples taken during the night were due to plant
respiration. Plants absorb CO during the day for photosynthesis to produce food, but at
night, they release CO back into the atmosphere. By studying these measurements over
the course of a few years, Dr. Keeling also identified a large seasonal pattern in the data.
He noticed that CO levels are actually higher in the spring when decomposing plant matter
releases CO into the air, and lowest in the autumn when plants have stopped taking in CO
for photosynthesis. This cyclical pattern is what gives the Keeling Curve its distinctive
sawtooth appearance. However, Dr. Keeling also observed that at the Mauna Loa
Observatory, global atmospheric CO concentrations were rising nearly every year.
Since its creation, the Keeling Curve has served as a visual representation of Keeling's data,
which scientists have continued to collect since his death in 2005. As shown in the figure,
in 2013, atmospheric CO concentrations surpassed the 400 parts per million mark.
According to the latest data from 2023, the atmospheric CO concentration has now
surpassed 420 parts per million, precisely around 421 parts per million. This increase is not
limited to CO alone. If we closely examine the atmospheric concentration of all
greenhouse gases, it becomes evident that the levels of these gases, including CO, are at
levels that are unprecedented in at least 800,000 years. This dramatic rise underscores the
significant impact human activities, particularly the use of fossil fuels, have had on the
Earth's climate system.

In fact, the concentrations of carbon dioxide, methane, and nitrous oxide have all shown
significant increases since 1750, which marks the beginning of the Industrial Revolution.
During this period, the increased burning of fossil fuels to meet growing energy demands,
along with rapid urbanization, changes in land use patterns, and intensified agricultural
activities, have all contributed to these rising levels.CO concentrations, in particular, are
increasing at the fastest rate ever observed. Additionally, after nearly a decade of stable
methane concentrations since the late 1990s, atmospheric measurements have indicated a
renewed increase in methane concentrations starting in 2007. This resurgence highlights
the ongoing challenges in managing and mitigating greenhouse gas emissions.
Although there has been a slight decrease in recent years due to several government
policies, overall, there has been an increase in CO concentrations, which can also be
attributed to forestry and other land use activities. Additionally, methane emissions from
agricultural activities, waste, and the energy sector have seen a significant rise, as have
nitrous oxide concentrations and the levels of fluorinated gases. All these factors
undeniably point to the fact that the current increase in greenhouse gas concentrations is
primarily due to human activities, specifically anthropogenic activities. The most
significant contributor is the burning of fossil fuels, particularly in the form of coal, oil,
and natural gas.

Now, to understand what is actually happening within the Earth's climate system as
greenhouse gas concentrations increase, we need to explore the concept of climate forcings.
So, what are climate forcings? Climate forcings are elements that are responsible for the
observed changes in the Earth's energy budget. Climate forcings are essentially the physical
processes that influence the Earth's climate. For example, these processes include the
reflection of sunlight by clouds, aerosols, and ice covers, as well as the absorption of heat
by the Earth's surface. Climate forcings impact the Earth's climate by driving changes
through various factors, which is why they are termed "forcings"--because they push the
climate towards change. It is important to understand that these forcings exist outside of
the current climate system. As discussed in the previous lecture, the Earth's climate system
comprises several components, including the hydrosphere, land surface, cryosphere,
biosphere, and atmosphere.
So, forcings are essentially components that drive the climate, but they exist outside of the
current climate system. For example, some of the most significant types of forcings include
variations in solar radiation levels, volcanic eruptions, changes in the Earth's albedo
(reflectivity), and fluctuating levels of greenhouse gases in the atmosphere. Each of these
is considered an external forcing because these events can independently alter the climate.
For instance, changes in solar activity or human-induced fossil fuel combustion are external
influences that drive changes in the climate system. Now, if we look into some of the basic
laws of thermodynamics, we can see how they relate to the Earth's energy balance. As the
Earth absorbs energy from the sun, it must also emit an equal amount of energy to maintain
balance, according to the law of conservation of energy--specifically, the first law of
thermodynamics. The Earth absorbs energy, and it must eventually emit an equal amount
of energy. The difference between the incoming and outgoing radiation is known as the
planet's radiative forcing. Just as applying a force to a physical object causes it to become
unbalanced and move, a climate forcing similarly forces the Earth's climate system to
change. When these forcings result in incoming energy being greater than outgoing energy,
the Earth will warm. Conversely, if the outgoing energy exceeds the incoming energy, the
planet will cool. So, climate forcings are, as I mentioned to you they are basically the
elements that are responsible for the observed change in the earth's energy budget.
This graph here illustrates the strength of the warming or cooling effects of various climate
forcings when compared to the Earth's equilibrium state before the Industrial Revolution.
It includes both natural and anthropogenic emissions. Anthropogenic emissions primarily
consist of greenhouse gases, aerosols, and changes in land use patterns, such as surface
albedo (the reflection of solar energy from the Earth's surface). On the other hand, natural
causes include factors like solar irradiance and volcanic activity. As mentioned earlier in
today's lecture, solar irradiance has not had a significant impact as a climate forcing in
recent times because the incoming solar radiation has remained relatively constant.

On the other hand, if we examine greenhouse gases, CO emissions since 1750 have
accumulated significantly in the atmosphere. Although a substantial portion of these
emissions has been absorbed by the oceans and terrestrial ecosystems, particularly forests,
the remaining concentration of CO is now responsible for about half of the current human-
caused global warming. This indicates that much of the warming is essentially irreversible.
Methane, which is the second-largest anthropogenic greenhouse gas or forcing currently
affecting the climate, also plays a crucial role. In addition to its well-known direct
greenhouse gas effects, methane acts as a precursor to other potent greenhouse gases, such
as ozone, water vapor, and carbon dioxide. This amplifies its overall impact on global
warming.

Therefore, methane also indirectly contributes to the warming of the Earth's atmosphere.
Next, we have fluorinated gases, or F-gases, which possess an extremely high capacity to
warm the Earth's atmosphere. These gases primarily originate from industrial and domestic
uses, particularly in refrigeration. Although most of them have been phased out due to the
Montreal Protocol, it will still take some time for them to gradually disappear from the
atmosphere. Despite this, their current presence continues to exert a forcing effect on the
Earth's climate system. Nitrous oxide is another potent and long-lived greenhouse gas, with
its concentration steadily rising, primarily due to intensive agricultural practices.

Atmospheric aerosols are small liquid or solid particles suspended in the Earth's
atmosphere, excluding clouds and precipitation particles. These aerosols can originate from
both natural processes and human activities, and they have the capacity to impact our
climate in several ways through their complex interactions with radiation and clouds.
Aerosols typically have a lifespan of one day to two weeks in the troposphere, and in some
cases, they can persist for about a year in the stratosphere. Aerosols also vary greatly in
size. When discussing natural sources of aerosols, they can originate from sources like
pollen, volcanic eruptions, and dust storms, manifesting as dust, dirt, and other particulates.
On the other hand, anthropogenic sources of aerosols primarily include soot from the
burning of fossil fuels, as well as exhaust emissions from automobiles and other human
activities.
Some aerosol particles, such as dust and sea spray, are primarily or entirely of natural
origin. Others, like sulfates and particulate matter present in smoke, originate from both
natural and anthropogenic sources. Aerosols can influence our climate in several ways.
Firstly, aerosols can scatter or absorb sunlight, thereby altering the Earth's radiative
balance. When aerosols scatter sunlight, they generally increase the planet's reflectivity,
which tends to cool the climate. Conversely, when aerosols absorb sunlight, they contribute
to warming, having the opposite effect. This dual role makes aerosols a complex factor in
climate dynamics.

Certain aerosols have the ability to absorb solar radiation, which can lead to a warming of
the climate system. The balance between the cooling and warming effects of aerosols
depends on the specific properties of the aerosols themselves, as well as the environmental
conditions in which they are present. In addition to their impact on temperature, aerosols
also play a critical role in cloud formation. They act as condensation and ice nucleation
sites, where cloud droplets and ice particles can form. The concentration of aerosols in the
atmosphere influences the extent of cloud cover or ice formation, which in turn increases
the reflection of incoming solar radiation, contributing to a cooling effect. This interaction
between aerosols and clouds adds another layer of complexity to their overall impact on
the climate system.

Now, if we examine the impact of aerosols as a climate forcing in this figure, we can
observe that carbon aerosols, which are essentially small carbon particles like soot resulting
from the incomplete combustion of fossil fuels and biomass, play a significant role. These
carbon aerosols can warm the atmosphere by absorbing solar radiation. Additionally, they
can contribute to warming the Earth's surface by covering snow, which reduces its albedo,
or reflectivity. Because these aerosols have a relatively short atmospheric lifetime of only
a few days, reducing their emissions can quickly decrease both near-term warming and
local air pollution. This makes targeting carbon aerosols a potentially effective strategy for
immediate climate and health benefits.

Next, we have sulfate aerosols. Sulfate aerosols are primarily emitted by fossil fuel power
plants and volcanic eruptions. These aerosols are known as cooling aerosols because they
reflect sunlight, thus having a cooling effect on the Earth's atmosphere. Their direct cooling
effect is relatively well understood, as they act like a veil at higher altitudes, essentially
providing shade and protecting us from sunlight.

We also have aerosol-cloud interactions, which I just mentioned. Aerosol-cloud
interactions influence the properties and formation of clouds, which can, in turn, alter
atmospheric motions and radiation properties. While the net effect of these interactions is
most likely cooling, it depends on various factors such as the type and altitude of the clouds
they affect.
Another important factor is land-use forcing. Land-use forcing refers to changes in land

use that modify the surface albedo. For example, deforestation typically increases the

reflection of sunlight because the open areas left behind are more likely to become covered

by  snow,  which  has  a  high  albedo..

If these areas are not covered by snow, they can actually act as a positive forcing,
contributing to warming rather than cooling. This shift occurs because the exposed land
absorbs more sunlight instead of reflecting it, which would have been the case if snow were
present.

Looking again at the Earth's atmosphere, it is evident that anthropogenic forcings have
been on the rise since the Industrial Revolution, accelerating particularly rapidly since the
1970s. The graph shown here illustrates the significant influence of human-caused forcings
over the past 150 years. This increased trend is primarily due to the buildup of long-lived
greenhouse gases in the atmosphere.

Conversely, the cooling effect of aerosols has stabilized because these emissions are
relatively short-lived. However, even if greenhouse gas emissions were to stabilize, the net
anthropogenic warming effect is expected to continue increasing. The cumulative effect of
radiative forcing over the years significantly impacts the Earth's energy balance and
average temperature.
When comparing these effects with natural forcings, it is clear that natural forcings have a
much less severe cumulative impact on the Earth's energy budget. For example, solar
forcing, as mentioned earlier, has remained relatively constant, indicating that the amount
of solar radiation reaching the Earth's surface has not changed significantly.

Large volcanic eruptions inject aerosols 10 to 30 kilometers into the stratosphere, where
they persist for 1 to 2 years. These aerosols have strong, but episodic, cooling effects by
reflecting sunlight away from the Earth. Overall, when we examine natural forcings, it
becomes clear that they have not been sustained at significant levels compared to
anthropogenic forcings. Consequently, the Earth's energy uptake, resulting from the
cumulative effect of total forcings since the Industrial Revolution, has predominantly been
driven by human activities. No other forcings have been proven or assessed so far to have
a comparable impact on the Earth's climate system.
It is also crucial to understand that our oceans have massive thermal inertia, meaning they
can significantly slow down surface warming, delaying the full impact by several centuries.
For instance, a sudden doubling of atmospheric CO concentration would create a positive
climate forcing, leading to an immediate energy imbalance. This imbalance would
gradually diminish as the Earth warms toward a new equilibrium. The time it takes for the
Earth's temperature to reach this equilibrium is referred to as the climate lag, which is
heavily influenced by the thermal inertia of the oceans.

In fact, two distinct phenomena occur in this context: rapid continent warming and slow
ocean warming. Approximately 10 years after any climate forcing event, surface warming
reaches about 40 to 50 percent of its final value. During this period, the lower atmosphere
warms rapidly, while the oceans remain cooler due to their substantial thermal inertia.

In fact, the heat exchange between the atmosphere and the shallow ocean layers can
temporarily cool the surface in the short run. This phenomenon occurs because the shallow
ocean layers absorb some of the heat from the atmosphere, reducing the immediate impact
on surface temperatures. When we talk about slow ocean warming, it's essential to
understand that it takes much longer for the Earth's surface temperature to adjust and
harmonize with the ongoing warming process. Over the long run, however, the surface and
oceans have enough time to exchange heat, eventually warming together at the same pace.

The total climate lag is primarily driven by the ocean's thermal inertia, which depends on
ocean dynamics, particularly how effectively the cold, deep layers of the ocean mix with
the hotter, shallow layers. Due to the ocean's massive thermal inertia, there is a noticeable
lag between the rate at which the surface warms and the rate at which the oceans warm.
This lag can delay the full effects of climate change, masking the immediate impact of
increased greenhouse gas concentrations on surface temperatures.

 To summarize the impact of climate forcings, we can observe that the analysis of natural
and human-induced climate forcings since the Industrial Revolution clearly indicates that
human activities have had a much more significant impact on the Earth's energy balance
than any natural forcings. Since 1750, the Earth has been forced to accumulate a substantial
amount of energy. The warming effects of human activities have steadily increased since
the onset of the Industrial Revolution, as the temporary cooling effects of short-lived
aerosols have been overshadowed by the more persistent greenhouse gases.
The energy accumulated by the Earth since the 1750s has largely been absorbed by the
Earth's oceans. However, due to the oceans' high thermal inertia, they take much longer to
warm up. This delay creates an extensive climate lag between the initial forcing and the
final surface warming, meaning the full effects of current greenhouse gas emissions will
not be immediately felt, but will continue to influence the climate for centuries to come.
Next, we will look into the global warming potential (GWP) of various greenhouse gases.
So far, we have seen that greenhouse gases warm the Earth's surface by absorbing the
energy reflected by the Earth's surface and slowing the rate at which this energy escapes to
space, essentially acting like a blanket around the planet. There are two key ways in which
these gases differ from one another. The first is their ability to absorb energy, known as
their radiative efficiency. The second is the duration they remain in the atmosphere, which
influences their long-term impact on global warming.

The global warming potential (GWP) was developed to compare the global warming
impact of different greenhouse gases. Specifically, GWP measures how much energy the
emission of one ton of a gas will absorb over a specific period, relative to the emission of
one ton of CO. By definition, carbon dioxide has a GWP of one, regardless of the time
period used, because it serves as the reference gas. CO remains in the atmosphere for a
very long time, making it a key contributor to long-term climate change.
Typically, the GWP is calculated over a 100-year period, although values for 20 years and
500 years are sometimes used. However, most policymakers and researchers focus on the
100-year timeline when assessing the impacts of different greenhouse gases on global
warming.
As I previously mentioned, CO has a global warming potential (GWP) of 1, as it is used
as the reference gas. Methane, on the other hand, is estimated to have a GWP of between
25 to 30 over a period of 100 years. While methane remains in the atmosphere for about a
decade on average, which is much less time than CO, it absorbs significantly more energy
than CO, contributing to its higher GWP.
Similarly, nitrous oxide has a GWP of 298 times that of CO over a 100-year time scale.
This means that nitrous oxide emitted today will remain in the atmosphere for more than
100 years on average, making it a potent long-term greenhouse gas. Additionally, gases
such as chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs), hydrofluorocarbons (HFCs), and sulfur hexafluoride
(SF) have even greater heat-trapping capacities than CO, further intensifying their impact
on global warming.
Although their global warming potential is higher, the atmospheric lifetime of gases like
chlorofluorocarbons and other F-gases is also substantially longer than that of CO. This
longevity makes it crucial to remove these gases from the Earth's atmosphere as soon as
possible to mitigate their long-term impact on global warming.

Now, you might wonder why we focus so much on CO emissions when other greenhouse
gases have a greater global warming potential. To understand this, we need to delve into
the global carbon cycle. The global carbon cycle can be represented as a series of carbon
reservoirs within the Earth system, interconnected by carbon fluxes, which describe the
movement of carbon between these reservoirs. Understanding this cycle is key to grasping
why CO, despite its lower GWP, remains a primary concern in climate discussions.
There are basically two distinct domains within the global carbon cycle: the fast domain
and the slow domain.
The fast domain includes carbon in the atmosphere, oceans, and land vegetation. It is
referred to as the "fast" domain because there is a rapid exchange of carbon dioxide among
these reservoirs. The turnover time for carbon in these reservoirs ranges from decades to
millennia, meaning that carbon cycles through these components relatively quickly.
On the other hand, the slow domain consists of the vast carbon reserves found in rocks and
sediments. This domain is termed "slow" because carbon exchange with the fast domain
occurs at a much slower rate. This exchange typically happens through processes such as
volcanic activity, erosion, and sedimentation. The turnover time in the slow domain spans
over 10,000 years, reflecting the much longer timescales involved in the movement of
carbon between these reservoirs.

Understanding these two domains is crucial for grasping how carbon cycles through the
Earth system and why different components of the carbon cycle operate on such varied
timescales.
Prior to the Industrial Revolution, the fast domain of the global carbon cycle was relatively
stable, maintaining a near-steady state. In the atmosphere, where carbon atoms
predominantly existed in the form of carbon dioxide and methane, the carbon fluxes were
balanced, and greenhouse gas concentrations remained approximately constant.
However, since the beginning of the industrial era, the extraction and combustion of fossil
fuels have introduced a significant amount of carbon dioxide into the atmosphere. This
process has effectively transferred a substantial amount of carbon from the slow domain,
where it was stored in fossil fuels, into the fast domain, thus altering the Earth's carbon
budget and fluxes within the fast domain.
It is important to understand that carbon, as a molecule, constantly cycles through various
reservoirs within the Earth's atmosphere, a process known as the global carbon cycle. This
cycle involves continuous flows of carbon in and out of the atmosphere and through living
organisms. For example, during photosynthesis, plants absorb carbon dioxide from the
atmosphere, converting it into biomass. When these plants die, the carbon stored in them
is transferred to the soil, where microorganisms decompose the organic matter, releasing
carbon back into the atmosphere.

This ongoing cycle is critical for maintaining the balance of carbon in the Earth's system,
but human activities, particularly since the Industrial Revolution, have significantly
disrupted this balance by introducing large quantities of carbon from the slow domain into
the fast domain.

These processes, such as the absorption of carbon by forests and oceans, are essentially the
Earth's natural "carbon sinks." Forests, for instance, act as significant carbon sinks because
they continuously remove carbon dioxide from the atmosphere through the process of
photosynthesis. Similarly, our oceans serve as another major carbon sink, absorbing a large
portion of atmospheric carbon dioxide.

On the other hand, any process that adds more carbon dioxide to the atmosphere is referred
to as a "carbon source." Today, the burning of fossil fuels--such as coal, oil, and natural
gas--serves as a primary carbon source, contributing significantly to the increase in
atmospheric CO levels. Additionally, activities like raising cattle for food also release
substantial amounts of carbon into the atmosphere, further contributing to the greenhouse
gas burden.

Despite the enormous amount of carbon dioxide emitted by fossil fuel combustion, a
considerable portion of it has been absorbed by these natural carbon sinks, namely our
oceans and forests. This absorption has somewhat mitigated the rate at which atmospheric
CO levels have risen, but the balance between carbon sources and sinks remains critically
disrupted due to ongoing human activities.

What we observe is a cumulative rise in the atmospheric concentration of CO. This rise
highlights a significant imbalance between carbon sources and carbon sinks. This
imbalance is further exacerbated as we continue to burn more fossil fuels and destroy
forests. The ongoing destruction of forests, combined with the relentless burning of fossil
fuels, continues to widen this gap.
