Continuing with the idea of the ecological footprint, we now have a concept based on the
same idea and it is called earth overshoot day.

 The global footprint network calculates the earth overshoot day based on determining
humanity's demand for ecological resources and services in a given year and what the earth
can regenerate. So, biocapacity per person is calculated, accounting for food, fiber, timber,
space for roads and structures, energy production whether it is from hydropower, biomass
whatever it is and waste absorption including CO2 emissions from fossil fuel or any other
activity like cement production and so on. Now as long as our ecological footprint is below
the biocapacity there is no issue But, when the date on which the ecological footprint on a
per capita basis exceeds the biocapacity then you have what is called the earth overshoot
day. So, the global ecological footprint on a per person basis is 2.

8 global hectares and the global biocapacity is 1.6 global hectares. So, I covered most of
these points in the previous lecture and just to give you an idea of what this means.

So, in 1971 earth overshoot day was at the end of December. So, what that means is that
in 1971 biocapacity and the ecological footprint were more or less equal not too different.
 So, within one year the ecological resources that were being utilized were within the
earth's capacity to regenerate them within a year. What has happened in the subsequent
years in the years after that is that the date is arriving sooner and sooner. So, we now
have a situation where let us look at when it landed for 2019. The earth overshoot day was
29th July 2019. In 2021 we have 30th July and in 2022 it was 28th July.

We have one of the last measures of environmental impacts that I will be talking about in
week one and that is life cycle assessment. Life cycle assessment was proposed by the US
EPA several decades ago as a methodology for determining environmental impacts of any
product or process.

 So here we have our inputs. As part of our inputs, we are using raw material, we are using
energy, air and water. Now in every step of the manufacturing process, all of these inputs
will have to be accounted for. So raw material extraction, whether you are extracting it
from mines or from any other resource, natural resource, all that extraction has to be
accounted for. Then we have transportation of the raw materials to the primary product
manufacturing sites and very often we notice in petroleum products, we notice this in paper
products and so many other industries that the primary product does not go directly to the
consumer. You may have secondary or higher levels of manufacturing.

 So, all of these secondary and higher levels of manufacturing, all these products at all
levels will then be packaged and distributed. What the consumer sees is going to be a
limited number of products. So that is the use. At the end of the life of that product, it can
be either reused or recycled. I will be using paper as an example of this entire exercise.
 So whether the product is reused or recycled at every step in the process, as I said, we are
utilizing mass, energy, air and water. We are also generating all kinds of waste. So, we
are generating gaseous emissions, wastewater emissions and we also have solid waste. So,
all these outputs also have to be accounted for and their impact on different parts of the
environment can be quantified.

 So, let us start with our example of paper. There are different types of paper in the market.
I am using four different types of paper as an example of this exercise in this particular
slide and in the next one. We do not see recycled paper here. I will come to that later. So,
when you manufacture paper, most paper manufacturers use wood from softwood as well
as hardwood trees.

 This is called virgin pulp. It has got three components to it. It has got bark, it has got
fiber and it has got lignin. De-barking is the first step in the process. So, removal of the
bark of the wood is the first process. What is interesting is that this de-barking process,
which is the bark of the wood, is usable as fuel in the next steps in the paper manufacturing
process.

 So, we have thermo-mechanical pulping where the fuel from the bark or rather the bark
is used as a fuel for thermo-mechanical pulping. What happens to the fiber and the lignin?
For good quality paper, lignin has to be removed. But if you are generating newsprint,
newsprint is considered the lowest grade other than recycled paper, newsprint out of virgin
pulp is considered the lowest grade of paper. So that does not have to be de-lignified.

 All the remaining types of paper have to be de-lignified. So, this fiber and lignin is taken
for thermo-mechanical pulping and then it goes for high pressure and high pressure, high
temperature chemical pulping is done in the next stage to get pure fiber. So, this is the pulp
that is then used for generating good quality paper and lignin is removed in the process.
Now as I said, thermo-mechanical pulping without de-lignification gives us newsprint and
the remaining processes after de-lignification are bleaching and in some cases coating. So,
bleaching is the next process. Then additives like clay and certain chemicals are added to
either give the paper a glossy look and so on.

 But if you are using a four-printer paper that has clay and certain chemicals, it is not
necessarily glossy paper but that is what we are going to look at in our next example. And
toilet paper which is again fine pulp, so very fine pulp which is sprayed as a mist on a metal
sheet is then removed to generate toilet paper. So, it does not have a coating, it is pure pulp
and it is bleached.
