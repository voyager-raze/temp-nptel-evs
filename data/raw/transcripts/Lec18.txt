Week 04 (Lecture 03): CLIMATE CHANGE (Projections and Related Impacts)

Welcome to lecture 3 of week 4 of the NPTEL online certification course on Environmental
Science. In the last lecture, we explored the key drivers of climate change, focusing on the
factors contributing to this global issue.
In this lecture, we will familiarize ourselves with the potential impacts of climate change.
Specifically, we will examine the long-term consequences of climate change on our polar
regions and high mountains, oceans and marine life, weather systems, livestock production
and food security, ecosystems, and overall well-being. As you may already understand,
climate change is set to impact nearly every sphere of our planet.

By the end of this lecture, you should be able to explain the following terminologies:
cascading impacts of climate change, permafrost, global sea level rise, ocean acidification,
extreme weather, among others.
We have already seen that human-induced warming has reached approximately 1 degree
Celsius above pre-industrial levels. In the decade from 2006 to 2015, the average global
temperature increase reached 0.87 degrees Celsius relative to the period between 1850 and
1900. This warming has been predominantly due to human activities, particularly the
increasing concentration of greenhouse gases in the atmosphere.

Given that global temperatures are currently rising by almost 0.2 degrees Celsius per
decade, human-induced warming likely reached 1 degree Celsius above pre-industrial
levels around 2017. If this pace of warming continues, it is projected to reach 1.5 degrees
Celsius around 2050. Since the 1970s, most land regions have been warming at a faster
rate than the global average.

This means that in many regions, warming has already exceeded 1.5 degrees Celsius above
pre-industrial levels. Over a fifth of the global population today lives in regions where at
least one season has already experienced warming greater than 1.5 degrees Celsius above
pre-industrial levels. The warming caused by anthropogenic emissions from the pre-
industrial period to the present is expected to persist for centuries to millennia, leading to
long-term changes in the climate system.
It is crucial to familiarize ourselves with the long-term adverse effects of climate change.
Widespread evidence indicates that climate change will have severe consequences for
much of the world, including higher temperatures, rising sea levels, increased heavy
rainfall, prolonged drought conditions, heat stress, inundation, and related damage, among
others. These impacts will not all manifest simultaneously or in the same manner. Some
will emerge abruptly, while others will develop slowly and continue over time.
Additionally, multiple impacts can occur concurrently and in various combinations,
complicating efforts to mitigate and adapt to these challenges.

The interactions between these climate change impacts will have significant spatial and
temporal implications, spreading across different settings and contexts. While the full
scope and scale of these effects are not yet fully understood, empirical evidence is
beginning to suggest that climate change impacts will propagate as cascades across both
physical and human systems. This means that changes in one system will inevitably affect
others due to the interconnected nature of these systems. For instance, the combined effects
of more frequent and intense storms, sea level rise, and increased storm tides can have
compounding impacts on the ability of individuals, governments, and the private sector to
adapt in time to prevent loss and damage. Therefore, it is urgently necessary to improve
our understanding of the extent, scope, and implications of these cascading changes to
better prepare and respond to the challenges posed by climate change.
As temperatures increase, there will be a significant rise in the melting of ice and snow.
Sea ice, which is a crucial component of the Arctic Ocean, undergoes seasonal changes in
its extent and thickness. During the dark winter months, sea ice covers almost the entire
Arctic Ocean. However, in the summer, some of this ice melts due to warmer temperatures
and extended daylight hours. Typically, sea ice reaches its minimum thickness and extent
by mid-September, with the area covered by ice being roughly half the size of its winter
maximum. This seasonal cycle of melting and freezing is being increasingly disrupted by
rising global temperatures, leading to thinner ice and a reduction in the overall area covered
by ice.
As the ice begins to expand again during the fall and winter, the extent of the area covered
by sea ice becomes a crucial indicator of changes in the global climate. Warmer air and
temperatures are steadily reducing the amount of ice that remains. Ice, being light-colored,
reflects more sunlight back into space than liquid water does. This reflective property,
known as albedo, plays a significant role in maintaining the Earth's energy balance. By
reflecting sunlight, sea ice helps keep the polar regions cool, contributing to the overall
stability of the planet's climate. As sea ice diminishes, less sunlight is reflected, leading to
further warming and creating a feedback loop that accelerates the melting of ice.

Sea ice plays a crucial role in maintaining cool air temperatures by acting as a barrier
between the cold air above and the relatively warmer water below. Climate change has the
potential to drastically alter the Earth's snow and ice-covered regions because snow and ice
can easily transition between solid and liquid states in response to even minor changes in
temperature.

Since 1992, the massive ice sheets that cover Greenland and Antarctica have lost more than
100 billion metric tons of ice per year on average. The area covered by ice is typically at
its smallest in September, following the summer melting season. Over time, the annual
minimum extent of Arctic sea ice has steadily decreased, highlighting the ongoing impact
of climate change on these vital regions. The shrinking ice cover not only contributes to
rising sea levels but also reduces the Earth's ability to reflect sunlight, further exacerbating
global warming.

In September 2021, the Arctic sea ice extent was recorded as the 12th smallest ever
observed. The length of the Arctic ice melt season has also extended, and the ice itself has
become thinner, making it more susceptible to further melting. As the amount of sea ice
continues to decrease, the cooling effect provided by the Arctic regions is diminished. This
reduction in sea ice not only contributes to global warming by decreasing the Earth's albedo
(the reflective capacity of the Earth's surface) but also leads to a feedback loop where less
ice results in more heat absorption, further accelerating the melting process and warming
the planet.

This process may trigger a feedback loop, where ocean warming--caused by increased
absorption of solar energy--leads to an even greater loss of sea ice, which in turn causes
further warming. This creates a cycle of diminishing snow and ice cover, causing the
Earth's surface to absorb more energy from the sun and become warmer.

The reduction in snowfall and snow cover diminishes the beneficial insulating effects of
snow, which not only affects vegetation and wildlife but also has broader implications for
water supplies, transportation, cultural practices, travel, and recreation for millions of
people around the world. The changing climatic conditions can have global consequences,
as snow and ice influence not only air temperature but also sea levels, ocean currents, storm
patterns, and ocean salinity.

For instance, the melting of ice sheets on Greenland and Antarctica adds fresh water to the
ocean, disrupting the balance and affecting these critical environmental factors. This
increase in sea levels can significantly alter ocean circulation patterns, which are driven by
variations in both temperature and salinity.

In addition to the melting of ice and snow, the Arctic and other cold regions have soils that
contain perennially frozen layers, known as permafrost. These soils in the northern
permafrost regions store a vast amount of organic carbon, estimated to be between 1,460
to 1,600 petagrams of carbon across surface soils and deeper deposits. Specifically,
permafrost soils and deposits hold approximately 1,070 to 1,360 petagrams of carbon. Of
this, around 300 to 400 petagrams are stored within the first meter of soil, while the
remainder is stored at greater depths.

The remaining carbon is stored in permafrost-free soils within the permafrost regions.
These carbon deposits have accumulated over thousands of years due to the slow rates of
organic matter decomposition in frozen or waterlogged soil layers. However, these frozen
soils are highly susceptible to decomposition once they thaw. A warming climate can
indeed cause some of this permafrost to thaw, releasing the stored carbon into the
atmosphere.

In fact, permafrost thawing has already been observed in many locations around the world.
This thawing process can lead to the release of greenhouse gases like carbon dioxide and
methane, further exacerbating global warming and creating a feedback loop that accelerates
climate change. A thawing permafrost layer can indeed have severe impacts on both people
and the environment. For instance, as the ice-filled permafrost thaws, it can turn into a
muddy slurry, which will no longer support the weight of the soil and vegetation above it.
This destabilization can lead to significant changes in the landscape, including the collapse
of buildings, roads, and other infrastructure built on what was once solid ground.
Permafrost plays a crucial role in the Arctic ecosystem by making the ground watertight,
thereby maintaining the vast network of wetlands and lakes across the Arctic tundra. These
wetlands are vital habitats for various plant and animal species, supporting a rich
biodiversity. However, the thawing of permafrost threatens to drain these wetland breeding
sites, which could have devastating consequences for numerous species that rely on these
environments for survival, particularly during the breeding season.
The loss of these habitats could lead to a decline in wildlife populations and disrupt the
ecological balance of the region, affecting not only the local environment but also the
global climate system. The release of greenhouse gases from thawing permafrost further
compounds these issues, contributing to an accelerated pace of climate change.

Additionally, as organic matter, such as the remains of plants currently frozen in the
permafrost, begins to thaw, it will start to decompose. This decomposition process will
release methane and carbon dioxide into the atmosphere, further contributing to global
warming. The cryosphere, as I mentioned earlier, includes not only snow, glaciers, and
permafrost but also lake and river ice, and it is an integral element of high mountain
regions.
High mountain regions are home to roughly 10 percent of the global population.
Widespread changes in the cryosphere will affect physical, biological, and human systems
in the mountains as well as the surrounding lowlands. For instance, glacier retreat and
permafrost thaw can decrease the stability of mountain slopes and the integrity of
infrastructure, while also increasing the number and area of glacier lakes. These changes
can lead to landslides, floods, and other cascading events in areas where such events have
not been previously recorded.

Snow avalanches are also projected to decline in number, and the run-out distance at lower
elevations will decrease. However, avalanches involving wet snow, even in winter, are
expected to occur more frequently. Additionally, rain-on-snow floods are anticipated to
occur earlier in spring and later in summer. In several regions, worsening route safety will
reduce mountaineering opportunities. The variability and decline in natural snow cover
will compromise the operation of low-elevation ski resorts, and the decline in glaciers and
snow will impact the aesthetic, spiritual, and cultural aspects of mountain landscapes.
These changes not only alter the natural environment but also affect the livelihoods,
traditions, and well-being of the communities that rely on these high mountain regions. The
cascading impacts of climate change in these areas highlight the interconnectedness of
physical and human systems, underscoring the need for comprehensive adaptation
strategies to address these challenges.
This situation will also negatively impact the well-being of people, especially in regions
such as the Himalayas, Eastern Africa, and the tropical Andes. As a consequence of both
natural and anthropogenic changes in the climate system, sea level changes are occurring
on temporal and spatial scales that threaten coastal communities, cities, and low-lying
islands.

When we refer to "sea level" in this context, we are talking about the time-averaged height
of the sea surface, which eliminates short-duration fluctuations like waves, tides, and
surges. The global mean sea level rise refers to an increase in the volume of ocean water.
After a period of approximately 2,000 years of little change, the global mean sea level has
risen faster in the 20th century than in any prior century over the last three millennia.
In fact, it has increased by about 15 centimeters. The global mean sea level rise has
accelerated since the late 1960s, with an average rate of 2.3 millimeters per year over the
period from 1971 to 2018, increasing to almost 3.7 millimeters per year over the period
from 2006 to 2018.

It is important to understand that global sea level rise is primarily a result of changes in the
volume of water in the oceans, which is largely driven by changes in ocean temperature,
melting, and increased discharge of land-based ice, such as glaciers, ice caps, and ice sheets
in Greenland and Antarctica, as well as changes in runoff, including dam construction or
groundwater withdrawal.
Ocean thermal expansion, which is caused by the excess heat being absorbed by the oceans,
and mass loss from glaciers have dominated the total change from 1901 to 2018. The
contribution of Greenland and Antarctica to the global mean sea level rise was actually
four times larger during the 2010 to 2019 period than during the 1992 to 1999 period. Due
to the increased ice sheet mass loss, the total loss of land ice was the largest contributor to
global mean sea level rise over the period from 2006 to 2018.
By 2050, sea levels are expected to rise an additional 10 to 25 centimeters, regardless of
whether or not greenhouse gas emissions are reduced. This projection highlights the urgent
need for adaptation and mitigation strategies to protect vulnerable regions from the impacts
of rising sea levels.

Even if net zero emissions are achieved, sea level rise will persist. This ongoing rise is
primarily due to the slow response of the deep oceans and ice sheets to environmental
changes. The deep oceans will continue to warm, and the ice sheets will gradually adjust
to the warming caused by past and present emissions. This slow response means that even
as we reduce emissions to net zero, the effects of previous emissions will continue to impact
sea levels.

Ocean and ice sheets are inherently slow to react to environmental changes. For example,
under low-emission scenarios, projections suggest that sea level rise will continue at a rate
similar to today's, even as we approach net zero emissions. However, in scenarios where
emissions remain high, projections indicate a substantial acceleration in sea level rise--
potentially more than five times the current rate by 2100. This acceleration could be
particularly pronounced if processes that expedite the retreat of the Antarctic ice sheet
become widespread.
Rising sea levels will undoubtedly increase the frequency and severity of extreme sea level
events along the coasts. The most commonly referenced risks associated with sea level rise
include increased coastal flooding from encroaching seas and waves, which could manifest
as both permanent inundation and episodic events caused by storms.

Moreover, rising seas will also trigger other natural processes along the coast that could
lead to negative impacts, such as erosion and rising groundwater levels. For instance, as
waves crash further up the shore, they will erode sand from coastal reef walls and beaches.
In coastal communities where the underground water table is already near the land surface,
higher ocean water levels could force groundwater levels up, leading to flooding.

The most immediate trigger for high sea level events is often a combination of high tide
and storm surge. These events can lead to sudden and severe flooding, particularly in low-
lying coastal areas, making it crucial to consider both immediate and long-term strategies
for managing and mitigating the risks associated with rising sea levels.

Sea levels essentially establish a baseline for storm surges. Storm surges are potentially
destructive increases in sea height that occur during coastal storms. As local sea levels rise,
this baseline also rises, allowing coastal storm surges to penetrate further inland. This
increased reach of storm surges can lead to more extensive flooding in coastal areas.

Beyond flooding, one of the key impacts of such sea level encroachments is the intrusion
of saltwater into coastal ecosystems and aquifers--a phenomenon known as saltwater
intrusion. This intrusion can have significant implications for freshwater resources,
agriculture, and natural habitats, making it an issue of growing concern as sea levels
continue to rise.
Due to the projected global mean sea level rise, extreme sea levels that are historically rare
will become more common by 2100 under all climate change scenarios. This means that
what were once considered rare and extreme events could become regular occurrences,
posing ongoing challenges for coastal communities and ecosystems.

 Many low-lying cities, such as Miami in Florida and Dhaka in Bangladesh, along with
small islands across various latitudes, are expected to experience significant sea level rise
and related extreme events annually by 2050. This is true regardless of whether these areas
are urban or rural, continental or island-based.
Beyond sea level rise, it's important to recognize the critical role that oceans play in
regulating the amount of carbon dioxide (CO) in the atmosphere. As atmospheric CO
concentrations increase, the oceans absorb more of this gas. However, due to the slow
mixing time between surface and deeper waters, it can take hundreds to thousands of years
to establish a new balance.
Over the past 250 years, the oceans have absorbed about 28 percent of the CO produced
by human activities. While this ability of the oceans to absorb CO prevents atmospheric
levels from rising even higher, the increasing levels of dissolved CO in the oceans can
negatively impact marine life.
CO reacts with seawater to form carbonic acid, which increases the acidity of the water.
This rise in acidity alters the balance of minerals in the ocean, particularly affecting the
availability of carbonate ions. These ions are crucial for marine organisms, such as corals
and shellfish, that rely on them to form their shells and skeletons.

The data shown here, derived from computer model simulations, depicts the surface ocean
aragonite saturation state from 1880 to 2015. This state is based on historical data and
future projections of CO emissions, with the locations of continents and coral reefs
marked. Aragonite saturation state is commonly used to track ocean acidification because
it provides a measure of carbonate ion concentration. This measure is crucial in
understanding how changes in ocean chemistry might affect marine ecosystems,
particularly coral reefs, which are highly sensitive to changes in aragonite levels.

Aragonite is one of the most soluble forms of calcium carbonate, widely utilized by marine
calcifiers, such as corals, mollusks, and other organisms that form calcium carbonate
structures. These organisms thrive and are more likely to survive and reproduce when the
aragonite saturation state is greater than 3. However, when the aragonite saturation level
falls below 3, these organisms become stressed, and when it drops below 1, the shells and
other aragonite structures begin to dissolve. This poses a significant threat to marine life,
particularly coral reefs, which are highly sensitive to changes in ocean chemistry.
The predicted decrease in pH by approximately 0.3 units during the 21st century represents
a potentially greater change than any seen in the last 300 million years. Such a shift could
have profound impacts on marine ecosystems, disrupting the delicate balance that many
organisms rely on for survival.

It's also important to understand that the air-sea gas exchange is a physicochemical process
primarily controlled by the difference in gas concentration between the air and sea, as well
as the exchange coefficient. This exchange coefficient determines how quickly a molecule
of gas, such as CO, can move across the ocean-atmosphere boundary. On average, it takes
about one year for CO in the surface ocean to equilibrate with atmospheric CO. This slow
process means that it's not uncommon to observe significant differences in CO
concentrations between the air and sea, often driven by variability in oceanic biological
activity and circulation patterns.
The oceans contain a vast reservoir of carbon that interacts with the atmosphere, as CO
reacts with water to form carbonic acid and its dissociation products, such as bicarbonate.
As atmospheric CO concentrations rise, these interactions with the surface ocean alter
seawater chemistry, leading to ocean acidification. Evidence suggests that past and current
ocean uptake of human-derived CO is primarily a physical response to these rising
atmospheric CO concentrations.

Whenever the partial pressure of a gas increases in the atmosphere over a body of water,
the gas diffuses into the water until the partial pressures across the air-water interface
equilibrate. However, the global carbon cycle is deeply intertwined with the physical
climate system, creating several feedback loops between the two systems. For instance,
increased CO levels modify the climate, which, in turn, impacts ocean circulation and
consequently the ocean's capacity to absorb CO.

In addition, changes in marine ecosystems due to rising CO levels or climate shifts can
also influence air-sea CO exchange. These feedback mechanisms complicate predictions
about how the ocean carbon cycle will function in the future.

The increased acidity of the ocean alters the balance of minerals in the water, making it
more challenging for corals and plankton to produce calcium carbonate, the primary
component of their hard exoskeletons and shells. Declines in coral and plankton
populations can lead to significant changes in marine ecosystems, ultimately affecting fish
populations and the people who rely on them for food and livelihood.

 And signs of such damage are already starting to appear in certain areas. Now, rising
global average temperatures are also associated with widespread changes in weather.
Scientific studies indicate that extreme weather events, such as heat waves and large
storms, are likely to become more frequent or more intense with human-induced climate
change. These extreme events are abrupt, occurring in the present, and are highly visible,
as opposed to long-term climate change trends that seem abstract, distant, gradual, and
complicated. Now today if you see the news, so the global news is in fact full of ,reports
on extreme weather or climate events on a regular basis. And long term changes in climate
can actually directly or indirectly affect many aspects of society in potentially disruptive
ways.

For example, warmer average temperatures could increase air conditioning costs. They can
also affect the spread of diseases like Lyme disease, malaria, and others. However, these
temperature changes could also improve conditions for growing certain crops. More
extreme variations in weather pose a significant threat to society. More frequent and
extreme heat events can lead to increased illness and deaths, especially among vulnerable
populations. Additionally, these events can damage crops and disrupt food production.
 While increased precipitation can replenish water supplies and support agriculture,
intense storms can damage property, cause loss of life, and lead to population displacement.
Such storms can also temporarily disrupt essential services, including transportation,
telecommunications, energy, and water supplies.
When considering the impacts of climate change on our water resources, extreme weather
conditions are projected to reduce renewable surface water and groundwater resources,
particularly in many dry subtropical regions. This reduction will likely intensify
competition for water among various sectors, further stressing these already vulnerable
areas.
In present dry regions, drought frequency is expected to increase by the end of the 21st
century. In contrast, water resources are likely to increase at high latitudes. Climate change
is projected to reduce both raw water quality and pose risks to drinking water quality, even
with conventional treatment, due to interacting factors. These factors include increased
temperature, elevated sediment, nutrient, and pollutant loadings from heavy rainfall, higher
concentrations of pollutants during droughts, and disruption of treatment facilities during
floods.

 When we look into our food supply system, crop productivity is projected to increase
slightly at middle to high latitudes with moderate atmospheric warming. However, this
productivity will decrease if warming becomes too severe. For example, moderately
warmer atmospheric temperatures and increased precipitation at northern latitudes may
lead to a northward shift of some agricultural production to parts of midwestern Canada,
Russia, and Ukraine. But overall food production could decrease due to unsuitable soils in
these northern regions. According to several projections, food may remain plentiful for a
while because of the longer growing season in the northern regions.
But most scientists warn that by 2050, some 200 to 600 million of the world's poorest and
most vulnerable people could face malnutrition and starvation due to the effects of climate
change on agricultural systems. In addition, livestock systems will also be impacted by
climate change, mainly through increasing temperatures, precipitation variation, and
atmospheric CO concentration, along with a combination of these factors. Temperature
affects most of the critical aspects of livestock production, such as water availability,
animal production and reproduction, and animal health. Livestock diseases are also likely
to be influenced by increased temperature and precipitation variations. In short, climate
change will reduce the availability of livestock products as well as their quality and safety
due to contamination with pathogens or pesticides, and it will also reduce the nutritional
quality and sensory appeal of these products.
Apart from that, when we look into our ecosystems, a large fraction of both terrestrial and
freshwater species face an increased extinction risk under the projected climate change
during and beyond the 21st century. This risk is particularly heightened as climate change
interacts with other stressors such as habitat modification, over-exploitation, pollution, and
invasive species. In fact, extinction risk increases under all climate change scenarios. As
seen in this graph, many species are projected to be unable to keep up with the pace of
climate change. The maximum speed at which they can move across land is slower than
the average climate velocity in their environment. This is particularly true in flat areas
where even small temperature changes correspond to larger geographical shifts.

In such scenarios, and in the absence of human intervention, tree mortality and associated
forest diebacks are expected to occur in many regions. Small mammals and primates will
also face significant risks, particularly in flat areas. Although marine species may have an
easier time shifting their ranges, invasion and warming could lead to high local extinction
rates, especially in tropical and semi-enclosed seas.

Lastly, when we consider the consequences of climate change on humans, the impacts are
broad and far-reaching. Changes in the Earth's climate can significantly affect public
health, agriculture, water supplies, energy production and use, land use and development,
recreation, and more. These shifts underscore the critical need for comprehensive strategies
to mitigate and adapt to the ongoing changes in our environment.

The nature and extent of these effects, and whether they will be harmful or beneficial, will
vary regionally as well as over time. Changes in climate will actually alter the average
weather conditions to which we are accustomed, resulting in multiple threats to human
health and welfare. Warmer average temperatures will continue to lead to hotter days and
more frequent, longer heat waves, increasing the number of heat-related illnesses and
deaths.

Additionally, increases in the frequency or severity of extreme weather events, such as
storms, will heighten the risk of dangerous flooding, high winds, and other direct threats to
people and property. Warmer temperatures will also reduce air quality by intensifying the
chemical reactions that produce photochemical smog. Along with changes in precipitation
patterns and extreme events, these factors could also enhance the spread of certain diseases.

In addition to all that, climate change will require adaptation on larger and faster scales
than in the past, presenting significant challenges to human well-being and the economy.
The more extensively and rapidly the climate changes, the more profound the potential
effects on society will be, influencing different regions and sectors to varying degrees.

And that depends not only on the sensitivity of those systems to climate change but also
on their ability to adapt or cope with it. Populations of particular concern include the poor,
children, the elderly, those already in poor health, the disabled, and indigenous populations.

To summarize today's lecture, we can see that climate change will have far-reaching
impacts on the planet as a whole. It will affect not only ecosystems and human well-being
but also nearly every sphere of the planet that comprises the environment we thrive in.
